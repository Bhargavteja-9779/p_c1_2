# Discussion {#sec:discussion}

## What the evidence supports

Three findings are robust across datasets, miscoverage levels, stratifications and learner families. First, marginal and covariate-adaptive conformal methods systematically under-cover rare targets: their worst-stratum coverage at a nominal 90% was {{marg.worst_min}}–{{marg.worst_max}} on average, and the null reference shows that this is not sampling noise. Proposition 1 and the synthetic study show that the shortfall is structural; better covariate adaptivity alone cannot remove it. Second, calibrating within target strata restores stratum-wise coverage for every score, as Mondrian theory guarantees and as BCCP [@randahl2026bin] demonstrated for one application; our benchmark shows that the guarantee holds in practice across {{n_datasets}} datasets, is robust to moderate target shift, and costs a median {{price.S-NCP.med}}–{{price.SDLC (proposed).med}}-fold increase in set size. Third, the choice of score matters for efficiency in the direction predicted by Theorem 2: the density-level score gave smaller stratum-valid sets than the residual score of BCCP, the quantile score of BFQR-style calibration and the HPD score, with both gradient-boosted and neural base models, and gave more targeted tail alerts.

Two qualifications are equally well supported. The advantage of the density score over the normalised residual depends on the quality of the scale model: it was clear with the perceptron, typical but not uniform with gradient boosting, and absent at $\alpha=0.05$, where few calibration points fall in the tail strata. And the efficiency of any density score depends on its implementation: evaluating density sets on a coarse grid of the marginal target distribution, as in our pre-registered implementation, inflated them enough to erase the advantage. Density-based conformal methods should therefore be evaluated at a resolution relative to the conditional scale.

## Practical guidance

For practitioners who must report how often extreme outcomes are covered, our results suggest the following. (i) Use stratified calibration whenever coverage of rare targets is a requirement; marginal coverage, however adaptive the score, does not deliver it. (ii) Choose strata from training-target quantiles so that each stratum receives at least several times $1/\alpha$ calibration points; otherwise the method returns whole strata. Finer strata extend protection to finer target ranges (Table [-@tbl:schemes]) at a modest cost in size. (iii) Use a density-level score: SDLC with a location–scale density is a robust default, and S-NCP is a close and simpler alternative when the scale model is noisy. (iv) When an interval is mandatory, report the hull; when marginal efficiency matters more than full stratum-wise validity, the floor variant protects every stratum at level $1-\beta$ while keeping marginal coverage $1-\alpha$. (v) Treat the sets as tail alerts: whenever the set reaches into the upper stratum, an extreme outcome is plausible, and the recall of such alerts is guaranteed.

## Limitations {#sec:limitations}

*Size cost and data requirements.* Stratum-wise validity is paid for in set size, and the payment grows when tail strata are small: at $\alpha=0.05$ or with a quarter of the calibration set, sets became considerably larger, and with fewer than $1/\alpha_k-1$ calibration points the method returns the whole stratum. Cross-fitted or jackknife+-type calibration [@barber2021jackknife] and pooling of sparse strata in the spirit of clustered conformal prediction [@ding2023class] are natural remedies that we did not evaluate; any pooling rule must be decided from training data only to preserve validity.

*Granularity of the guarantee.* The guarantee holds for the calibrated strata, not for arbitrary sub-ranges: within a stratum, coverage can be lower near its outer end (deciles 7–9 in Fig. [-@fig:deciles]), and continuous tilts that move mass within a stratum reduce coverage (Table [-@tbl:shift]). Corollary 1 quantifies this, but the bound is vacuous for open tail strata under unbounded tilts.

*Density estimation.* The optimality of Theorem 2 is an oracle property. The location–scale density cannot represent atoms (zero inflation), multimodality or covariate-dependent shape beyond scale, and the exploratory two-piece variant did not improve on it. On the zero-inflated forest-fire data SDLC was less efficient than BCCP. Flexible conditional density estimators (mixture density networks, normalising flows, distributional boosting) may close the gap to the oracle and are an obvious next step.

*Evaluation scope.* The benchmark covers independent tabular data with targets having at least 20 distinct values; ordinal, count and temporally dependent targets were excluded. Several datasets contain duplicate rows (up to {{T:S2_leakage_audit|dataset=sarcos|dup_rows_frac|pct}} for sarcos); random splitting keeps the data exchangeable, so the guarantees are unaffected, but point accuracy may be optimistic. All results are for split calibration with a 50/25/25 split and a single fixed configuration of the base learners, chosen without tuning to avoid favouring any method. Finally, three implementation decisions were changed after pre-registration (Section [-@sec:protocol]); the pre-registered results are reported in the supplementary material, the coverage conclusions are identical, and only the size comparison of density-based scores depended on the implementation.

*Applicability of the guarantee.* Like all conformal guarantees, stratum-wise coverage requires exchangeability of calibration and test data; it holds on average over calibration sets and test points of a stratum, not conditionally on a particular covariate value, and it does not protect against shifts of the covariate distribution within strata.
