# Discussion {#sec:discussion}

## What the evidence supports

Three findings stand out. The first two held in every analysis; the third held on most datasets but not on small or strongly skewed targets or under grouped splits. First, marginal and covariate-adaptive methods systematically under-cover rare targets (worst-stratum coverage {{marg.worst_stratum.min}}–{{marg.worst_stratum.max}} at a nominal 90%), beyond sampling noise, and the oracle analysis shows that this is not an artefact of estimation. Second, calibrating within target strata restores stratum-wise coverage for every score, as Mondrian theory guarantees; the guarantee was preserved exactly when a target shift re-weighted whole strata, stratified methods lost less coverage than marginal ones under continuous tilts, and the guarantee cost a {{price.S-NCP.geo}}–{{price.BCCP.geo}}-fold size increase, less than lowering $\alpha$ for a marginal method. Third, the density score gave smaller stratum-valid sets than the residual score of BCCP, the quantile score and the HPD score on most datasets, consistent with Proposition 2, and the ordering was confirmed on {{conf.n_eval}} held-out datasets under a pre-specified plan.

Two qualifications are equally well supported. The gain over the information-matched normalised residual is a few percent and comes mainly from reallocating coverage from noisy to quiet inputs; its price is lower coverage of the noisiest inputs, occasional empty sets and lower coverage of the uncalibrated top 1%. And the estimated location–scale density is far from the oracle when the noise shape changes with $x$. The ranking of scores is a statement about average efficiency, not a free improvement.

## Practical guidance

For practitioners who must report how often extreme outcomes are covered, we suggest the following.

1. *Decide which guarantee is required.* Marginal coverage, however adaptive the score, does not protect rare targets, and lowering $\alpha$ is a less efficient substitute. If every target range must be covered at the nominal level, calibrate within strata (SDLC or S-NCP). If coverage averaged over the target range is required, or the most extreme outcomes beyond any practical stratum matter most, use RW-DLC with an inverse-density relevance; it covered the top 1% at close to the nominal level, at the price of lower coverage in the bulk.
2. *Choose strata from training-target quantiles* so that each stratum receives several times $1/\alpha$ calibration points; otherwise use fewer strata or the floor variant.
3. *Use a score that includes the scale $\hat\sigma(x)$.* SDLC, S-NCP and S-HPD gave stratum-valid sets within a few percent of each other; SDLC was smallest on most datasets, while S-NCP is simpler, spreads coverage more evenly across noise levels, covers more of the extreme 1% and gives cheaper alerts. The residual score of BCCP and stratified CQR were clearly less efficient. When an interval is required, the hull of S-NCP was as small as the hull of SDLC on held-out data.
4. *Report hulls when an interval is mandatory.* Stratified sets can be discontiguous: SDLC's sets had {{T:D2_main|Method=SDLC|Pieces|f2}} pieces on average (BCCP {{T:D2_main|Method=BCCP|Pieces|f2}}), mostly an isolated piece in a tail stratum whose threshold is lower than that of its neighbour (Fig. [-@fig:example]). Such a piece says that an extreme outcome is plausible, but an interval is easier to communicate. SDLC-hull is valid and smaller than BCCP-hull, but loses part of the advantage.
5. *For yes/no decisions about extremes, match the rule to the costs.* A class-conditional conformal tail classifier attains the same recall guarantee with fewer alerts and is the better choice when false alerts are expensive; among set-based alerts, S-NCP's were the cheapest. Set-based alerts have higher recall, are cheaper when misses are very costly, and carry more information about the size of the outcome.

## Limitations {#sec:limitations}

*Size cost and data requirements.* Stratum-wise validity is paid for in set size, more so for skewed targets and small tail strata; with fewer than $1/\alpha_k-1$ calibration points the method returns the whole stratum. Pooling sparse strata [@ding2023class], cross-fitted calibration [@barber2021jackknife] and overlapping groups [@bairaktari2025kandinsky] are natural remedies that we did not evaluate.

*Granularity of the guarantee.* The guarantee holds for the calibrated strata, not for arbitrary sub-ranges. Within a stratum, coverage is lower near its outer end (Remark A.1 in Appendix A), and shifts that move mass within a stratum reduce coverage (Table [-@tbl:shift]); Corollary 1 explains the direction of this effect, but its bounds are loose for open tail strata. The guarantee is also marginal over $X$ within each stratum, so it says nothing about a particular input, and the density score deliberately trades coverage across inputs.

*Density estimation and baselines.* Proposition 2 is an oracle property, and Proposition 3 requires a uniformly consistent density estimate, which the location–scale model provides only when the noise shape does not depend on $x$; flexible conditional density estimators may close the gap to the oracle. We did not stratify further covariate-adaptive methods such as conditional histograms [@sesia2021conformal] or localised conformal prediction [@hore2025local]; they cannot beat the oracle density score, but their estimated versions may be competitive.

*Evaluation scope.* The benchmark covers tabular targets with at least 20 distinct values; the rule excluded ordinal targets with few levels, but count, heavily discretised, coded and time-indexed targets were retained (Section [-@sec:data]). The held-out replication covered datasets with 4,209 to 50,000 rows, six with curator-supplied log-scale targets and none with the extreme skewness of some CTR23 targets, so it does not test the regimes in which the CTR23 size advantage disappeared (small or strongly skewed targets). All splits are random splits of one pool: the guarantees and the efficiency results hold for new rows from the same pool, not for new units or future periods. Five held-out datasets are hourly or daily series, and under grouped splits coverage varied widely between calibration sets (for video transcoding, SDLC's worst-stratum coverage ranged from {{J:statistics_review05.json|grouped.video/SDLC/worst_min|f2}} to {{J:statistics_review05.json|grouped.video/SDLC/worst_max|f2}} over seeds; Supplementary Table S15). All results use split calibration and one untuned base-learner configuration. The density-based implementation changed after earlier results had been seen (Section [-@sec:protocol]), which is why the CTR23 size comparisons are exploratory and were replicated on held-out data.

*Applicability of the guarantee.* Stratum-wise coverage requires exchangeability of calibration and test data, holds on average over calibration sets and over the test points of a stratum, and does not protect against covariate shift within strata.
