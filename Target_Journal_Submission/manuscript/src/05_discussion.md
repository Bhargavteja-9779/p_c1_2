# Discussion {#sec:discussion}

## What the evidence supports

Three findings are robust across datasets, miscoverage levels, stratifications and learner families. First, marginal and covariate-adaptive conformal methods systematically under-cover rare targets. Their worst-stratum coverage at a nominal 90% was {{marg.worst_stratum.min}}–{{marg.worst_stratum.max}} on average, and the null reference shows that this is not sampling noise. The oracle analysis (Section [-@sec:problem]) and the synthetic study show that the shortfall is not an artefact of estimation, although its size depends on the data-generating process. Second, calibrating within target strata restores stratum-wise coverage for every score, as Mondrian theory guarantees and as BCCP [@randahl2026bin] showed in simulations and one application. In our benchmark the guarantee held across {{n_datasets}} datasets, resisted moderate target shift, and cost a geometric-mean size increase of {{price.S-NCP.geo}}–{{price.BCCP.geo}}-fold. It is also the efficient way to protect the tails: lowering $\alpha$ for a marginal method bought less tail coverage at a larger size. Third, the score matters for efficiency in the direction predicted by Proposition 2. The density score gave smaller stratum-valid sets than the residual score of BCCP, the quantile score and the HPD score on most datasets, and the synthetic study confirms the ordering with known densities. The size ordering was confirmed on {{conf.n_eval}} held-out datasets under a pre-registered analysis plan.

Two qualifications are equally well supported. The gain of the density score over the information-matched normalised residual is small, a few per cent, and it comes mainly from the factor $1/\hat\sigma(x)$, that is, from reallocating coverage from noisy to quiet inputs. Its price is lower coverage of the noisiest inputs, occasional empty sets and lower coverage of the most extreme 1% of outcomes, which is not calibrated. Moreover, the estimated location–scale density is far from the oracle when the noise shape changes with $x$: in the bimodal synthetic design the oracle density set was much smaller than any estimated stratified set. The ranking of scores is therefore a statement about efficiency on average, not a free improvement.

## Practical guidance

For practitioners who must report how often extreme outcomes are covered, the results suggest the following.

1. *Calibrate within strata* whenever coverage of rare targets is a requirement. Marginal coverage, however adaptive the score, does not deliver it, and lowering $\alpha$ is a less efficient substitute.
2. *Choose strata from training-target quantiles* so that each stratum receives at least several times $1/\alpha$ calibration points; otherwise the method returns whole strata. Finer strata extend protection to narrower target ranges at a modest cost in size. With small calibration sets, use fewer strata or the floor variant.
3. *Choose the score by the secondary property that matters.* SDLC gives the smallest stratum-valid sets on average. S-NCP is almost as small, simpler, and spreads coverage more evenly across noise levels and into the extreme 1%. BCCP and stratified CQR were the least efficient.
4. *Report hulls when an interval is mandatory.* Stratified sets can be discontiguous: SDLC's sets had {{main.SDLC-LS.pieces}} pieces on average (BCCP {{main.S-Res.pieces}}), mostly an isolated piece in a tail stratum whose threshold is lower than that of its neighbour (Fig. [-@fig:example]). Such a piece is informative, because it says that an extreme outcome is plausible, but an interval is easier to communicate. SDLC-hull is valid and remains smaller than BCCP-hull, but it loses part of the advantage.
5. *For yes/no decisions about extremes, match the rule to the costs.* A class-conditional conformal tail classifier attains the same recall guarantee with fewer alerts and is the better choice when false alerts are expensive. Set-based alerts have higher recall, are cheaper when misses are very costly, and carry more information about the size of the outcome.

## Limitations {#sec:limitations}

*Size cost and data requirements.* Stratum-wise validity is paid for in set size, and the payment grows with skewness and when tail strata are small. With a quarter of the calibration set, sets became considerably larger, and with fewer than $1/\alpha_k-1$ calibration points the method returns the whole stratum. Pooling sparse strata, as in clustered conformal prediction [@ding2023class], cross-fitted calibration [@barber2021jackknife] and overlapping or weighted groups as in Kandinsky conformal prediction [@bairaktari2025kandinsky] are natural remedies that we did not evaluate. Any pooling rule must be decided from training data only to preserve validity.

*Granularity of the guarantee.* The guarantee holds for the calibrated strata, not for arbitrary sub-ranges. Within a stratum, coverage is lower near its outer end, and shifts that move mass within a stratum reduce coverage (Table [-@tbl:shift]); Corollary 1 explains the direction of this effect, but its bounds are loose for open tail strata. The guarantee is also marginal over $X$ within each stratum, so it says nothing about a particular input, and the density score deliberately trades coverage across inputs.

*Density estimation and baselines.* The optimality of Proposition 2 is an oracle property, and Proposition 3 requires a uniformly consistent density estimate, which the location–scale model provides only when the noise shape does not depend on $x$. Flexible conditional density estimators (mixture density networks, normalising flows, distributional boosting) may close the gap to the oracle. We did not run several covariate-adaptive methods that could be stratified in the same way, such as conditional histograms [@sesia2021conformal], conditional calibration [@gibbs2025conformal] or localised conformal prediction [@hore2025local]; Proposition 2 implies that none of them can beat the oracle density score, but their estimated versions may be competitive.

*Evaluation scope.* The benchmark covers tabular data with targets having at least 20 distinct values; ordinal, count and temporally dependent targets were excluded. Several datasets contain repeated units (duplicate rows in sarcos, repeated videos, repeat sales, team-season panels). Random splitting keeps the rows exchangeable, so the guarantees hold for new rows from the same pool, but not for new units or future periods. All results are for split calibration with a 50/25/25 split and one fixed configuration of the base learners, chosen without tuning to avoid favouring any method. The implementation of the density-based methods changed after earlier results had been seen (Section [-@sec:protocol]); this is why the size comparisons on CTR23 are labelled exploratory and were replicated on held-out data.

*Applicability of the guarantee.* Like all conformal guarantees, stratum-wise coverage requires exchangeability of calibration and test data. It holds on average over calibration sets and over the test points of a stratum, and it does not protect against shifts of the covariate distribution within strata.
