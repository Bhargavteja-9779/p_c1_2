# Introduction {#sec:intro}

Many regression problems that intelligent systems are asked to solve are judged by how they handle the *rare* outcomes: the unusually expensive property, the exceptional salary, the extreme load on a power grid, the very toxic compound. These outcomes are rare by definition, so they are under-represented in training data, and models trained by minimising average loss shrink their predictions towards the bulk of the target distribution. This is the setting of *imbalanced regression* [@branco2016survey; @ribeiro2020imbalanced; @yang2021delving], where a growing literature re-weights, re-samples or re-parameterises models so that point predictions for rare targets become more accurate.

Point predictions are only half of what a decision maker needs. Prediction *sets* (or intervals) with a coverage guarantee state how far the truth may be from the prediction, and conformal prediction delivers such guarantees in finite samples for any model, under exchangeability only [@vovk2005alrw; @lei2018distribution; @angelopoulos2023gentle]. Its standard guarantee, however, is *marginal*: the set contains the outcome with probability $1-\alpha$ *on average over all outcomes*. When rare outcomes are what matter, the relevant question is different: **when the outcome is extreme, how often does the set contain it?** A marginal guarantee is silent on this question, because the frequent bulk of the distribution can be over-covered while the rare tails are under-covered and the average still equals $1-\alpha$.

A large body of work makes conformal sets adapt to the covariates, so that coverage holds approximately conditionally on $X$ [@romano2019conformalized; @chernozhukov2021distributional; @izbicki2022cdsplit; @gibbs2025conformal]. A natural expectation is that such covariate-adaptive sets also protect rare outcomes. We show that they do not, and that this is not a matter of estimation quality: even the *oracle* set with exact coverage $1-\alpha$ at every $x$ covers only a fraction of the extreme outcomes, because an extreme outcome typically combines an extreme signal with an extreme, unpredictable noise realisation that no $X$-measurable set can anticipate. Coverage *conditional on the target*, which we call stratum-wise coverage, is therefore a different property that must be targeted explicitly.

Calibrating conformal thresholds separately within bins of the observed outcome is the natural device for this purpose, and it is not new. @randahl2026bin proposed it as bin-conditional conformal prediction (BCCP) for the heavily skewed distribution of conflict fatalities, and it is the binning step of the binned fair quantile regression of @wang2023equal, which equalises coverage across groups conditionally on the outcome. Its finite-sample bin-wise validity is an instance of Mondrian (label-conditional) validity [@vovk2005alrw; @vovk2013conditional; @toccaceli2019combination]. What has been missing is an explanation of *why* such calibration is unavoidable, guidance on *which* conformity score it should be combined with, a precise account of *what* it guarantees when the target distribution shifts, and evidence of *how much* it costs and gains across many problems. This paper supplies these four elements:

1. **Why covariate adaptivity is not enough.** We formalise stratum-wise coverage, $P\{Y\in C(X)\mid Y\in B_k\}\ge 1-\alpha_k$, for target strata $B_k$ such as the lower and upper tails; it is the regression analogue of class-conditional coverage. We prove that covariate-conditional validity does not imply it, even for the oracle: in a Gaussian model the shortest interval with coverage $1-\alpha$ at every $x$ covers only {{prop1.upper10_r05}} of the top-decile outcomes when the predictor explains half of the variance, and its coverage at $y$ vanishes as $|y|$ grows (Proposition 1). Tail under-coverage of standard conformal sets has been reported empirically [@randahl2026bin; @boruah2026distribution]; Proposition 1 shows that it persists with a perfect model.
2. **Which score to stratify.** Stratified calibration is valid for any score, so the score determines only how large the sets are. We prove that, among all prediction sets with coverage at least $1-\alpha_k$ in every stratum, expected Lebesgue measure is minimised by conditional-density level sets with stratum-specific thresholds (Theorem 2). This extends the Neyman–Pearson characterisation of least-ambiguous set-valued classifiers with class-specific coverage [@sadinle2019least; @lei2014classification] from finitely many labels to strata of a continuous outcome, and reduces to the known optimality of density level sets for marginal coverage [@lei2013distribution; @izbicki2022cdsplit] when $K=1$.
3. **SDLC and a precise account of its guarantees.** Stratified density-level conformal prediction (SDLC) combines stratified calibration with an estimate of the optimal score of Theorem 2, a semiparametric location–scale conditional density whose noise density is estimated by a kernel. A *floor* variant guarantees marginal coverage $1-\alpha$ and stratum-wise coverage $1-\beta$ simultaneously in finite samples. We make the often-invoked robustness to target shift precise (Corollary 1): stratum-wise coverage is preserved exactly when a shift re-weights whole strata, and otherwise it can degrade by at most the total-variation distance between the within-stratum laws, which we bound for label shift.
4. **A pre-registered benchmark.** On all {{n_datasets}} eligible datasets of the OpenML-CTR23 suite [@fischer2023openml], each with {{n_seeds}} predefined random splits, we compare {{n_methods_main}} conformal procedures that share identical base models, including BCCP in its union and hull forms, a BFQR-style binned conformalised quantile regression and six marginal or covariate-adaptive methods. Dataset-level non-parametric statistics quantify tail coverage, the price of stratum-wise validity in set size, whether the size advantage predicted by Theorem 2 survives estimation of the density, robustness to target shift, sensitivity to design choices, a second learner family and computational cost. Deviations from the pre-registered protocol are documented, and code, configuration and all raw results are released.
