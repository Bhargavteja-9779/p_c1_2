# Introduction {#sec:intro}

Many regression problems that intelligent systems solve are judged by how they handle *rare* outcomes: the unusually expensive property, the exceptional salary, the extreme load on a power grid, the very toxic compound. Rare outcomes are under-represented in training data, and models trained by minimising average loss shrink their predictions towards the bulk of the target distribution. This is the setting of *imbalanced regression* [@branco2016survey; @ribeiro2020imbalanced; @yang2021delving], where a growing literature re-weights, re-samples or re-parameterises models so that point predictions for rare targets become more accurate.

Point predictions are only half of what a decision maker needs. Prediction *sets* with a coverage guarantee state how far the truth may be from the prediction, and conformal prediction delivers such guarantees in finite samples for any model, under exchangeability only [@vovk2005alrw; @lei2018distribution; @angelopoulos2023gentle]. Its standard guarantee, however, is *marginal*: the set contains the outcome with probability $1-\alpha$ *on average over all outcomes*. When rare outcomes matter, the relevant question is different: **when the outcome is extreme, how often does the set contain it?** A marginal guarantee is silent on this, because the frequent bulk can be over-covered while the rare tails are under-covered.

Much work makes conformal sets adapt to the covariates, so that coverage holds approximately conditionally on $X$ [@romano2019conformalized; @chernozhukov2021distributional; @izbicki2022cdsplit; @gibbs2025conformal]. Such sets do not protect rare outcomes, and better estimation would not change this: even the *oracle* set with exact coverage $1-\alpha$ at every $x$ under-covers the tails, because an extreme outcome typically combines a large signal with a large, unpredictable noise realisation that no $X$-measurable set can anticipate. Coverage *conditional on the target*, which we call stratum-wise coverage, must be targeted explicitly.

Calibrating conformal thresholds separately within bins of the observed outcome is the natural device, and it is not new. @randahl2026bin proposed it as bin-conditional conformal prediction (BCCP) for conflict fatalities, and @wang2023equal calibrate within outcome bins to equalise coverage across groups. Its bin-wise validity is an instance of Mondrian (label-conditional) validity [@vovk2005alrw; @vovk2013conditional]. Two questions remain open. First, the guarantee holds for any score, so efficiency is decided by the score, and it is not known which score makes stratum-valid sets smallest or what that efficiency costs. Second, strata protect rare outcomes only at their own resolution: the most extreme outcomes lie inside a tail stratum and are covered only on average with it, while finer strata leave too few calibration points. Imbalanced regression usually describes the importance of an outcome by a continuous *relevance function* [@torgo2007utility; @ribeiro2020imbalanced; @steininger2021denseweight], and long-tailed classification has obtained conformal guarantees that average coverage over classes [@ding2025longtailed; @bhattacharyya2026macro]. We address both questions and, above all, what a practitioner should do. Our contributions are four.

1. **Diagnosis.** Standard and covariate-adaptive conformal intervals under-cover rare outcomes on almost every dataset, and so does the oracle covariate-conditional interval (Proposition 1: {{prop1.upper10_r05}} of the top decile in a Gaussian model with half of the variance explained), so better models cannot remove the shortfall.
2. **The price of tail validity.** Calibrating within target strata restores coverage in every stratum for any score at a size cost of about {{price.SDLC.geo}}, smaller than that of lowering the miscoverage level for all outcomes, and remains exact when the shift re-weights whole strata.
3. **Size-optimal scores for two tail guarantees.** Conditional-density level sets with stratum-specific thresholds are the smallest stratum-valid sets (Proposition 2, the per-stratum form of known level-set results [@lei2013distribution; @sadinle2019least]); SDLC is their plug-in method. For *relevance-weighted coverage*, the average of coverage weighted by a relevance function, relevance-weighted calibration is valid in finite samples (Theorem 2, weighted conformal prediction with known weights) and level sets of relevance times density are size-optimal (Proposition 4); RW-DLC implements them. Its sets resemble those of conformal prediction under an estimated target shift [@yi2025distribution], and relevance-weighted coverage is a single-weight member of the coverage families of Kandinsky conformal prediction [@bairaktari2025kandinsky]; what is new is the criterion for imbalanced regression and its evaluation. Empirically, among scores that use the estimated scale the choice changes set sizes by only a few percent, and we report this as a finding.
4. **A decision guide.** We compare which guarantee to choose, how to choose strata, sets versus intervals, and set-based alerts versus conformal tail classifiers and conformal selection [@jin2023selection] under explicit costs (Section [-@sec:discussion]).

Both methods decide coverage exactly at the observed target and compute set geometry on a fine grid, at a cost linear in the number of test points. The evidence comes from all {{n_datasets}} eligible OpenML-CTR23 datasets with {{n_seeds}} predefined splits, where we compare the stratified methods with BCCP, stratified normalised-residual, quantile and HPD scores and seven marginal or covariate-adaptive methods, with ablations of each score component, and from {{conf.n_datasets}} held-out datasets analysed under two pre-specified plans. We also analyse robustness to target shift, imbalance-aware training, a second learner family, and grouped and temporal splits.
