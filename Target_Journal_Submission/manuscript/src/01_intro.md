# Introduction {#sec:intro}

Expert systems that price, insure, inspect or allocate resources are increasingly required to report not only a prediction but how far the truth may be from it. Automated property valuation, salary benchmarking, load forecasting for power grids and toxicity screening share one feature: the costly errors occur on *rare, extreme* outcomes. An understated valuation of an expensive house misprices a mortgage or a tax assessment; an underestimated peak load or toxicity is a safety problem. These outcomes are rare by definition, so they are under-represented in training data, and models trained by minimising average loss shrink their predictions towards the bulk of the target distribution. This is the setting of *imbalanced regression* [@branco2016survey; @ribeiro2020imbalanced; @yang2021delving], where a growing literature re-weights, re-samples or re-parameterises models so that point predictions for rare targets become more accurate.

The problem is easy to overlook because standard uncertainty reports look correct on average. In the King County house-sales data (one split of our benchmark), a split-conformal 90% interval around a gradient-boosting model covered {{J:case_study_dollars.json|kings_county/SCP/coverage|pct}} of all test sales but only {{J:case_study_dollars.json|kings_county/SCP/top_coverage|pct}} of the {{J:case_study_dollars.json|kings_county/n_top|int}} sales in the top tenth of the training prices (at least \${{J:case_study_dollars.json|kings_county/top_threshold|int}}). The prices it missed exceeded its upper bound by a median of \${{J:case_study_dollars.json|kings_county/SCP/median_shortfall|int}}. Conformalised quantile regression covered {{J:case_study_dollars.json|kings_county/CQR/top_coverage|pct}} of these sales. Calibrating within target strata, the approach studied here, covered {{J:case_study_dollars.json|kings_county/SDLC/top_coverage|pct}} (Section [-@sec:case]).

Point predictions are only half of what a decision maker needs. Prediction *sets* (or intervals) with a coverage guarantee state how far the truth may be from the prediction, and conformal prediction delivers such guarantees in finite samples for any model, under exchangeability only [@vovk2005alrw; @lei2018distribution; @angelopoulos2023gentle]. Its standard guarantee, however, is *marginal*: the set contains the outcome with probability $1-\alpha$ *on average over all outcomes*. When rare outcomes are what matter, the relevant question is different: **when the outcome is extreme, how often does the set contain it?** A marginal guarantee is silent on this question, because the frequent bulk of the distribution can be over-covered while the rare tails are under-covered and the average still equals $1-\alpha$. For an expert system the natural requirement is outcome-conditional and auditable: *among the cases whose outcome turns out to be extreme, the prediction set must contain the outcome at a stated rate.* An auditor can check this requirement from held-out outcomes alone, without access to the model.

A large body of work makes conformal sets adapt to the covariates, so that coverage holds approximately conditionally on $X$ [@romano2019conformalized; @chernozhukov2021distributional; @izbicki2022cdsplit; @gibbs2025conformal]. One might expect such sets also to protect rare outcomes. They do not, and better estimation would not change this: even the *oracle* set with exact coverage $1-\alpha$ at every $x$ under-covers the tails of the target, because an extreme outcome typically combines a large signal with a large, unpredictable noise realisation that no $X$-measurable set can anticipate. Coverage *conditional on the target*, which we call stratum-wise coverage, is a different property that must be targeted explicitly.

Calibrating conformal thresholds separately within bins of the observed outcome is the natural device for this purpose, and it is not new. @randahl2026bin proposed it as bin-conditional conformal prediction (BCCP) for the heavily skewed distribution of conflict fatalities, and @wang2023equal calibrate within bins of the outcome to obtain equal coverage across groups. Its finite-sample bin-wise validity is an instance of Mondrian (label-conditional) validity [@vovk2005alrw; @vovk2013conditional]. What has been missing is the evidence and the theory that a system designer needs to adopt the device. Is the requirement really unmet by covariate-adaptive methods, or only by poorly estimated ones? Because the guarantee holds for any score, which score makes stratum-valid sets smallest, where does the gain come from and what does it cost? How does the guarantee behave when the target distribution shifts, and is a prediction set the right tool when the decision is a simple alert? Finally, do the answers hold beyond the data on which they were found? This paper answers these questions with four contributions. Stratified calibration itself is not one of them.

1. **Theory of stratum-wise coverage.** We show that the requirement cannot be met by improving covariate-conditional coverage, and we quantify how far the oracle covariate-conditional set falls short in the tails: in a Gaussian model with half of the variance explained, it covers {{prop1.upper10_r05}} of the top-decile outcomes (Proposition 1). Monte Carlo extensions to heavy-tailed signals and skewed noise show that the shortfall persists but that its shape depends on the model. For the oracle problem, conditional-density level sets with stratum-specific thresholds minimise expected size among all sets with stratum-wise coverage (Proposition 2), the per-stratum form of known level-set results [@lei2013distribution; @sadinle2019least; @kiyani2024length]. We prove that their plug-in conformal version is asymptotically size-optimal under uniform consistency of the density estimate (Proposition 3). We also show that, in a location–scale model, the gain over a normalised residual comes from reallocating coverage from high-noise to low-noise inputs, which explains both the efficiency and its cost. A corollary states when the known label-shift robustness of label-conditional calibration holds exactly for continuous outcomes and how it degrades otherwise.
2. **A method.** Stratified density-level conformal prediction (SDLC) combines stratified calibration with a semiparametric location–scale density whose noise distribution is estimated nonparametrically from cross-fitted residuals. It has an exact, stratum-exact implementation whose cost is linear in the number of test points.
3. **A large benchmark with a confirmatory replication.** On all {{n_datasets}} eligible OpenML-CTR23 datasets with {{n_seeds}} predefined splits, we compare {{n_methods_main}} conformal procedures that share identical base models. They include BCCP, stratified versions of the normalised-residual, quantile and HPD scores, an ablation that isolates each component of the density score, and seven marginal or covariate-adaptive methods. The size comparisons are then replicated on {{conf.n_datasets}} held-out datasets under an analysis plan that was made public before evaluation.
4. **Decision-oriented evidence for deployment.** We evaluate tail alerts derived from prediction sets against dedicated conformal tail classifiers and conformal selection [@jin2023selection], with expected-cost curves for valuation problems, and we analyse robustness to target shift, imbalance-aware training, a second learner family, the miscoverage level, the strata and the calibration-set size. A valuation case study expresses the consequences in money, and a five-point deployment guide (Section [-@sec:discussion]) states when to use SDLC, a simpler stratified score or a dedicated tail classifier.
