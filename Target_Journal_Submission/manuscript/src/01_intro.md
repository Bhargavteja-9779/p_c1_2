# Introduction {#sec:intro}

Many regression problems that intelligent systems are asked to solve are judged by how they handle the *rare* outcomes: the unusually expensive property, the very large insurance claim, the extreme load on a power grid, the exceptionally toxic compound. These outcomes are rare by definition, so they are under-represented in training data, and models trained by minimising average loss shrink their predictions towards the bulk of the target distribution. This is the setting of *imbalanced regression* [@branco2017survey; @ribeiro2020imbalanced; @yang2021delving], where a growing literature re-weights, re-samples or re-parameterises models so that point predictions for rare targets become more accurate.

Point predictions are only half of what a decision maker needs. Prediction *sets* (or intervals) with a coverage guarantee state how far the truth may be from the prediction, and conformal prediction delivers such guarantees in finite samples for any model, under exchangeability only [@vovk2005algorithmic; @lei2018distribution; @angelopoulos2023gentle]. Its standard guarantee, however, is *marginal*: the set contains the outcome with probability $1-\alpha$ *on average over all outcomes*. When rare outcomes are what matter, the relevant question is different: **when the outcome is extreme, how often does the set contain it?** A marginal guarantee is silent on this question, because the frequent bulk of the distribution can be over-covered while the rare tails are under-covered and the average still equals $1-\alpha$.

A large body of work makes conformal sets adapt to the covariates, so that coverage holds approximately conditionally on $X$ [@romano2019conformalized; @chernozhukov2021distributional; @izbicki2022cdsplit; @gibbs2025conformal]. A natural expectation is that such covariate-adaptive sets also protect rare outcomes. We show that they do not, and that this is not a matter of estimation quality: even the *oracle* set with exact coverage $1-\alpha$ at every $x$ covers only a fraction of the extreme outcomes, because an extreme outcome typically combines an extreme signal with an extreme, unpredictable noise realisation that no $X$-measurable set can anticipate (Proposition 1). Coverage *conditional on the target*, which we call target-conditional or stratum-wise coverage, is therefore a different property that must be targeted explicitly.

This paper studies target-conditional coverage for regression and makes the following contributions.

1. **Problem formulation and an impossibility result.** We formalise stratum-wise coverage, $P\{Y\in C(X)\mid Y\in B_k\}\ge 1-\alpha_k$ for target strata $B_k$ (e.g. the upper and lower tails), and prove that covariate-conditional validity does not imply it: in a Gaussian model the oracle covariate-conditional interval covers only {{prop1.upper10_r05}} of the top-decile outcomes when the predictor explains half of the variance (Proposition 1).
2. **A method with finite-sample guarantees.** We propose *target-stratified conformal prediction*, which calibrates a separate conformal threshold within each target stratum and assigns every candidate value the threshold of *its own* stratum. For any score it guarantees stratum-wise coverage in finite samples (Theorem 1) and, as a consequence, remains valid under arbitrary shifts of the target-stratum probabilities (Corollary 1).
3. **Optimal sets and the SDLC procedure.** We characterise the smallest sets with stratum-wise coverage: they are conditional-density level sets with stratum-specific thresholds (Theorem 2), extending the Neyman–Pearson reasoning of least-ambiguous classifiers [@sadinle2019least] to regression. This motivates SDLC (stratified density-level conformal), which combines stratified calibration with a semiparametric location–scale density estimate, together with a *floor* variant that keeps marginal coverage $1-\alpha$ while guaranteeing a lower floor in every stratum.
4. **A pre-registered large-scale evaluation.** On all {{n_datasets}} eligible datasets of the OpenML-CTR23 regression suite with {{n_seeds}} predefined repetitions, we compare {{n_methods_main}} conformal procedures that share identical base models, test the hypotheses with dataset-level non-parametric statistics, and analyse the *price* of stratum-wise validity in set size, its sensitivity to design choices, its behaviour under target shift and its computational cost. Code, configuration and all raw results are released.
