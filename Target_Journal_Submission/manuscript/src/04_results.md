## Marginal and covariate-adaptive methods under-cover the tails (RQ1)

All methods attained their nominal marginal coverage. Coverage of the target strata, however, differed sharply. The seven marginal or covariate-adaptive methods covered {{marg.upper_tail_cov.min}}–{{marg.upper_tail_cov.max}} of the upper-tail targets and {{marg.top1_cov.min}}–{{marg.top1_cov.max}} of the top 1%, and their worst-stratum coverage was {{marg.worst_stratum.min}}–{{marg.worst_stratum.max}}. On {{marg.below.min}}–{{marg.below.max}} of the {{n_datasets}} datasets, it was below the range expected under exact validity. Fig. [-@fig:deciles] shows the pattern: marginal methods over-cover the central deciles and pay for it in the outer ones, as Section [-@sec:problem] predicts. Covariate adaptivity reduces the shortfall (NCP, DCP and HPD-split are better than SCP and CQR) but does not remove it. Every stratified method, in contrast, covered {{strat.upper_tail_cov.min}}–{{strat.upper_tail_cov.max}} of the upper tail, with worst-stratum coverage {{strat.worst_stratum.min}}–{{strat.worst_stratum.max}}, and was below the null range on at most {{strat.below.max}} datasets, as expected under exact validity. SDLC's worst-stratum coverage exceeded that of each marginal method on at least {{h1w.minwins}} of {{n_datasets}} datasets (one-sided Holm-adjusted $p\le$ {{h1w.maxp}}; Hodges–Lehmann differences {{h1w.hlmin}}–{{h1w.hlmax}}). The result is unchanged without the development datasets ($p\le$ {{h1wx.maxp}}) and for upper-tail coverage alone ($p\le$ {{h1u.maxp}}). Protection extends partly beyond the calibrated strata: SDLC covered {{main.SDLC-LS.top5_cov.pct}} of the top 5% and {{main.SDLC-LS.top1_cov.pct}} of the top 1%, against at most {{marg.top5_cov.max}} and {{marg.top1_cov.max}} for the marginal methods. The top 1% is not calibrated, however, and its coverage remains below the nominal level for every method.

Marginal methods (SCP, CQR, HPD-split) over-cover the centre and under-cover the outer deciles; stratified calibration (S-NCP, SDLC) flattens the profile. The residual dip in deciles 2 and 9 lies inside the broad shoulder strata.}}

## The price of stratum-wise validity (RQ2)

Stratum-wise validity enlarges the sets (Table [-@tbl:price]). Holding the score fixed, stratified calibration increased the geometric-mean size by factors of {{price.S-NCP.geo}} (normalised residual) to {{price.BCCP.geo}} (absolute residual). For SDLC relative to the single-threshold density level set (DLS) the factor was {{price.SDLC.geo}} ({{price.SDLC.lo}}–{{price.SDLC.hi}}), with a median of {{price.SDLC.med}}, for a gain in worst-stratum coverage of {{price.SDLC.gain}} (between {{price.gain.min}} and {{price.gain.max}} across scores). The price was concentrated: it exceeded 1.5 on only {{price.SDLC.n15}} datasets for SDLC (Section [-@sec:error]).

A fairer view of the price compares stratified sets with marginal sets that buy tail coverage by lowering $\alpha$ (Table [-@tbl:matched]). At $\alpha=0.05$ every marginal method still had a lower worst-stratum coverage ({{mc.SCP.worst}}–{{mc.DCP.worst}}) than SDLC at $\alpha=0.1$ ({{main.SDLC-LS.worst_stratum}}), and SDLC's sets were smaller than theirs on {{mc.nsmaller.min}}–{{mc.nsmaller.max}} of the {{n_datasets}} datasets. Raising the overall coverage level is therefore an inefficient way to protect the tails: calibrating within strata gives more tail coverage for less size.

## The density score gives the smallest stratum-valid sets (RQ3)

Among the stratified methods, which all had equivalent worst-stratum coverage (TOST with margin 0.01: $p\le$ {{tost.S-NCP}} for every comparison with SDLC), SDLC produced the smallest sets (Table [-@tbl:size], Fig. [-@fig:tradeoff]). Its sets were smaller than those of BCCP on {{h3.BCCP.wins}} of {{n_datasets}} datasets (geometric-mean ratio {{h3.BCCP.geo}}, 95% CI {{h3.BCCP.lo}}–{{h3.BCCP.hi}}; Holm-adjusted $p$ {{h3.BCCP.p}}), than those of stratified CQR on {{h3.S-CQR.wins}} (ratio {{h3.S-CQR.geo}}) and than those of S-HPD on {{h3.S-HPD.wins}} (ratio {{h3.S-HPD.geo}}). Against the information-matched S-NCP, which uses the same $\hat\mu$ and $\hat\sigma$, SDLC was smaller on {{h3.S-NCP.wins}} datasets (Holm $p$ {{h3.S-NCP.p}}; Hodges–Lehmann ratio {{h3.S-NCP.hlr}}), but the gain was small and its geometric-mean ratio, {{h3.S-NCP.geo}} ({{h3.S-NCP.lo}}–{{h3.S-NCP.hi}}), was pulled towards one by a few datasets with large losses (Section [-@sec:error]). When an interval is required, SDLC-hull was smaller than BCCP-hull (ratio {{h3h.BCCP-hull.geo}}, {{h3h.BCCP-hull.wins}}/{{h3h.BCCP-hull.losses}}), whereas against S-NCP-hull the median favoured SDLC-hull ({{h3h.S-NCP-hull.wins}}/{{h3h.S-NCP-hull.losses}}, Hodges–Lehmann ratio {{h3h.S-NCP-hull.hlr}}) and the geometric mean did not ({{h3h.S-NCP-hull.geo}}). Because the implementation of the density-based methods was changed after earlier results had been seen (Section [-@sec:protocol]), we treat these comparisons as exploratory; Section [-@sec:confirmatory] tests them on held-out data.

The advantage was robust to the design choices (Supplementary Table S5). SDLC remained smaller than all four stratified comparators without the ten development and diagnostic datasets, on the {{n_nominal}} datasets whose realised tail strata are close to nominal, at $\alpha=0.05$ and $\alpha=0.2$, and with three or ten strata (Holm $p\le$ {{sens.robust.maxp}} in each of these analyses). Two subsets were less clear. On the {{sens.small.S-NCP.n}} datasets with fewer than 1,600 observations no stratified score was consistently better (SDLC vs S-NCP: {{sens.small.S-NCP.wins}}/{{sens.small.S-NCP.losses}}). On the {{sens.skewed.S-NCP.n}} strongly skewed datasets ($|$skewness$|>1$), SDLC was smaller than BCCP and S-HPD, while its advantage over S-NCP ({{sens.skewed.S-NCP.wins}}/{{sens.skewed.S-NCP.losses}}, Holm $p$ = {{sens.skewed.S-NCP.p}}) and S-CQR ($p$ = {{sens.skewed.S-CQR.p}}) was not significant.

All comparisons on CTR23 are exploratory (Section [-@sec:protocol]).}}

*Where the gain comes from.* The ablation chain in Table [-@tbl:size] and Fig. [-@fig:decomp] attributes the gain. Adding the scale $\hat\sigma(x)$ to the residual score (BCCP → S-NCP) reduced size on average (ratio {{dec.S-NCP_vs_BCCP.geo}}) but not consistently ({{dec.S-NCP_vs_BCCP.wins}}/{{dec.S-NCP_vs_BCCP.losses}} datasets, $p$ = {{dec.S-NCP_vs_BCCP.p}}). Replacing the normalised residual by a Gaussian density score, which only adds the factor $1/\hat\sigma(x)$ (S-NCP → SDLC-N), reduced size on {{dec.SDLC-N_vs_S-NCP.wins}} of {{n_datasets}} datasets ($p$ {{dec.SDLC-N_vs_S-NCP.p}}). Estimating the noise shape by the kernel density (SDLC-N → SDLC) added a further, smaller and less consistent reduction (ratio {{dec.SDLC-N.geo}}, {{dec.SDLC-N.wins}}/{{dec.SDLC-N.losses}}, $p$ = {{dec.SDLC-N.p}}). The two-piece density did not improve on the symmetric one ({{var.SDLC-LS2.size_geo}} vs {{main.SDLC-LS.size_geo}}), and the histogram density, whose resolution is limited by its 40 bins, was far less efficient ({{var.SDLC-H.size_geo}}). The size advantage of SDLC over S-NCP is therefore mostly the coverage reallocation described in Section [-@sec:method]: the density score widens the sets of low-noise inputs and narrows those of high-noise inputs.

*What the gain costs.* The reallocation has a visible price, which the theory predicts and which a user should weigh. The worst coverage over deciles of $\hat\sigma(x)$ was {{main.SDLC-LS.sigma_decile_worst}} for SDLC against {{main.S-NCP.sigma_decile_worst}} for S-NCP (Fig. [-@fig:sigma]). SDLC returned empty sets for {{main.SDLC-LS.empty_pct}} of test points (S-NCP {{main.S-NCP.empty_pct}}), concentrated on the noisiest inputs of high-$R^2$ datasets. SDLC also covered fewer of the most extreme outcomes than S-NCP and S-HPD: {{main.SDLC-LS.top1_cov}} of the top 1% against {{main.S-NCP.top1_cov}} and {{main.S-HPD-LS.top1_cov}} (Holm $p$ = {{ext.ext1_cov_2.S-NCP.p}} and {{ext.ext1_cov_2.S-HPD.p}}). It covered more than BCCP and S-CQR, however ({{main.S-Res.top1_cov}} and {{main.S-CQR.top1_cov}}). Within the calibrated strata these differences cancel, so that stratum-wise coverage is the same for all stratified methods.

## Synthetic study with known densities {#sec:synthetic}

The synthetic study separates the score from its estimate (Table [-@tbl:synthetic]). It covers five designs with 5,000 training and calibration points each: skewed location–scale noise (A), covariate-dependent skewness (B), covariate-dependent bimodality (C), heavy tails (D) and a zero-inflated outcome without a density (E). With the true density, the oracle SDLC set was smaller than the oracle normalised residual in every design. The gain ranged from {{J:metrics_v3.json|synthetic_oracle_gain_min|pct}} to {{J:metrics_v3.json|synthetic_oracle_gain_max|pct}}, and the largest gain occurred for the bimodal design C, where the density level set excludes the gap between the modes. The estimated SDLC was smaller than S-NCP in {{J:metrics_v3.json|synthetic_est_lt_sncp|int}} of the 50 design-seed combinations and smaller than BCCP in {{J:metrics_v3.json|synthetic_est_lt_bccp|int}}. Its sets were nevertheless much larger than the oracle's when the location–scale model was misspecified: for design C they were {{J:metrics_v3.json|synthetic_gap_C|pct}} larger than the oracle SDLC set. The exactly covariate-conditional oracle (O-HDR-X) covered only {{J:metrics_v3.json|synthetic_hdrx_upper_A|f3}} of the upper stratum in design A but {{J:metrics_v3.json|synthetic_hdrx_upper_C|f3}} in the bimodal design C, which illustrates that the size of the shortfall depends on the model (Section [-@sec:problem]). With only 500 training and calibration points, SDLC was smaller than S-NCP in {{J:metrics_v3.json|synthetic_small_lt_sncp|int}} of 20 combinations, with smaller gains. Under a genuine label shift, in which $X\mid Y$ is unchanged (Supplementary Table S10), all stratified methods degraded similarly as the shift moved mass within the upper stratum. At $\gamma=1$ in design A they covered {{T:T12_synthetic_label_shift|dgp=A&gamma=1.0&method=BCCP|upper|f3}}–{{T:T12_synthetic_label_shift|dgp=A&gamma=1.0&method=S-NCP|upper|f3}} of the upper stratum, close to the oracle SDLC ({{T:T12_synthetic_label_shift|dgp=A&gamma=1.0&method=O-SDLC|upper|f3}}), while the marginal methods fell to {{T:T12_synthetic_label_shift|dgp=A&gamma=1.0&method=HPD-split|upper|f3}}–{{T:T12_synthetic_label_shift|dgp=A&gamma=1.0&method=SCP|upper|f3}}.

## Confirmatory replication on held-out datasets {#sec:confirmatory}

On the {{conf.n_eval}} held-out datasets, analysed exactly as pre-registered (Supplementary Section S6), all four hypotheses were supported (Table [-@tbl:confirmatory]).

* *C-H1 (tail coverage).* SDLC's worst-stratum coverage exceeded that of each of the seven marginal and covariate-adaptive methods (Holm $p$ {{C-H1.maxp}}).
* *C-H2 (validity).* Its worst-stratum coverage fell below the 5% quantile of the exact-validity null reference on {{C-H2.below}} of {{conf.n_eval}} datasets (critical value {{C-H2.crit}}).
* *C-H3 (set size).* SDLC's sets were smaller than those of BCCP (geometric-mean ratio {{C-H3.BCCP.geo}}, {{C-H3.BCCP.wins}}/{{C-H3.BCCP.losses}} datasets, Holm $p$ = {{C-H3.BCCP.p}}), S-CQR ({{C-H3.S-CQR.geo}}, {{C-H3.S-CQR.wins}}/{{C-H3.S-CQR.losses}}, $p$ {{C-H3.S-CQR.p}}), S-NCP ({{C-H3.S-NCP.geo}}, {{C-H3.S-NCP.wins}}/{{C-H3.S-NCP.losses}}, $p$ = {{C-H3.S-NCP.p}}) and S-HPD ({{C-H3.S-HPD.geo}}, {{C-H3.S-HPD.wins}}/{{C-H3.S-HPD.losses}}, $p$ = {{C-H3.S-HPD.p}}). By the pre-registered rule, every comparison favours SDLC. The gains over S-NCP and S-HPD are again small and consistent: the bootstrap intervals of their geometric-mean ratios ({{C-H3.S-NCP.lo}}–{{C-H3.S-NCP.hi}} and {{C-H3.S-HPD.lo}}–{{C-H3.S-HPD.hi}}) include one, while the signed-rank and sign tests show that the reduction occurs on most datasets.
* *C-H4 (equivalence).* The worst-stratum coverage of SDLC was equivalent to that of S-NCP and BCCP within 0.02 (TOST $p$ {{C-H4.S-NCP}} and {{C-H4.BCCP}}).

## Target shift (RQ4)

Table [-@tbl:shift] and Fig. [-@fig:shift] report coverage under shift. Under the positive control, which re-weights whole strata, the stratum-wise coverage of every stratified method was unchanged, as Corollary 1(i) states. Their marginal coverage stayed at the nominal level ({{T:T8_shift|method=SDLC|pc_marginal|f3}} for SDLC), whereas that of the marginal methods fell to {{T:T8_shift|method=SCP|pc_marginal|f3}}–{{T:T8_shift|method=Mondrian-ŷ|pc_marginal|f3}}. Continuous tilts towards the upper tail also re-weight targets *within* strata, so Corollary 1(i) does not apply. Under a moderate tilt ($\gamma=1$) the stratified methods kept {{T:T8_shift|method=BCCP|up1_upper|f3}}–{{T:T8_shift|method=S-NCP|up1_upper|f3}} coverage of the upper stratum, against {{T:T8_shift|method=SCP|up1_upper|f3}}–{{T:T8_shift|method=DCP|up1_upper|f3}} for the marginal methods. Under a strong tilt ($\gamma=2$) every method lost coverage. The stratified methods lost less ({{T:T8_shift|method=S-CQR|up2_upper|f3}}–{{T:T8_shift|method=S-NCP|up2_upper|f3}}, against {{T:T8_shift|method=SCP|up2_upper|f3}}–{{T:T8_shift|method=DCP|up2_upper|f3}}). Among them, SDLC ({{T:T8_shift|method=SDLC|up2_upper|f3}}) was below S-NCP and S-HPD ({{T:T8_shift|method=S-NCP|up2_upper|f3}}), which is the same reallocation away from the most extreme outcomes seen above. The effective sample size of the $\gamma=2$ weights was small (median {{ess_up2_median}}, minimum {{ess_up2_min}}). Restricting the analysis to the {{shift_n_ess_ge_50}} datasets with an effective sample size of at least 50 left the ordering unchanged. The bounds of Corollary 1(ii)–(iii) are not informative for these unbounded tilts. The synthetic study (Section [-@sec:synthetic]) adds a genuine label shift with known $X\mid Y$.

## Tail alerts and decisions (RQ5)

A set that intersects the upper stratum raises an alert. Every stratified method guarantees an alert recall of at least 0.9; their mean recall was {{alert.strat.recall.min}}–{{alert.strat.recall.max}}, and it was at least 0.9 on all {{n_datasets}} datasets (Table [-@tbl:alerts]). The price of this conservativeness is a high alert rate. Among the stratified methods SDLC raised fewer alerts than BCCP ({{al.hi.BCCP.wins}}/{{al.hi.BCCP.losses}} datasets) and S-CQR, but more than S-NCP ({{al.hi.S-NCP.wins}}/{{al.hi.S-NCP.losses}}). A dedicated conformal tail classifier built from the same location–scale model (TC-LS) attains the guaranteed recall almost exactly (mean {{T:T13_tail_alerts|side=hi&rule=TC-LS|recall|f3}}) and raised fewer alerts than SDLC on every dataset (SDLC/TC-LS alert-rate ratio {{al.hi.TC-LS.geo}}, SDLC lower on {{al.hi.TC-LS.wins}} of {{n_datasets}} datasets). For a pure yes/no decision about extremes, a dedicated classifier is therefore the more efficient tool. Conformal selection [@jin2023selection] controls the false discovery rate instead and flagged few units, with high precision (mean {{T:T13_tail_alerts|side=hi&rule=CS-BH-0.2|precision|f3}} at $q=0.2$) but a mean recall of only {{T:T13_tail_alerts|side=hi&rule=CS-BH-0.2|recall|f3}}; the naive point-prediction rule behaved similarly (recall {{T:T13_tail_alerts|side=hi&rule=Point|recall|f3}}).

Which rule is preferable depends on the cost of a missed extreme outcome relative to a false alert (Table [-@tbl:alerts]). With equal costs, the rules that raise few alerts were cheapest: the point rule and conformal selection (expected cost {{T:T13_tail_alerts|side=hi&rule=Point|cost_r1|f3}} and {{T:T13_tail_alerts|side=hi&rule=CS-BH-0.2|cost_r1|f3}} per unit, against {{T:T13_tail_alerts|side=hi&rule=SDLC|cost_r1|f3}} for SDLC). When a miss costs ten times a false alert, SDLC's expected cost did not differ significantly from that of TC-LS (SDLC lower on {{al.hi.cost.TC-LS.wins}} of {{n_datasets}} datasets, $p$ = {{al.hi.cost.TC-LS.praw}}) and was lower than that of conformal selection and of the point rule on {{al.hi.cost.CS-BH-0.2.wins}} and {{al.hi.cost.Point.wins}} datasets. At a cost ratio of 20, SDLC was cheaper than TC-LS on {{al.hi.cost20.TC-LS.wins}} datasets ($p$ = {{al.hi.cost20.TC-LS.praw}}), because its recall exceeds the guaranteed 0.9 while TC-LS attains it almost exactly. Alerts read off *marginal* sets (SCP, CQR, HPD-split) were the cheapest on average at high cost ratios ({{T:T13_tail_alerts|side=hi&rule=HPD-split|cost_r20|f3}} for HPD-split at $r=20$). They had no recall guarantee, however, and their recall fell below 0.9 on {{alert.marg.below09.min}}–{{alert.marg.below09.max}} datasets. Fig. [-@fig:costs] shows the cost curves for three valuation problems (house prices in King County and Miami, wages in the CPS). The guarantee-free point rule and conformal selection become the most expensive rules as the cost ratio grows, and set-based alerts with stratum-wise coverage are the cheapest at the highest ratios. A set also carries more information than an alert: it says how large the outcome may be, which a downstream decision such as a provision, an inspection priority or a price range can use.

## Sensitivity analyses (RQ6) {#sec:robustness}

*Miscoverage level and strata.* The conclusions held at $\alpha=0.05$ and $\alpha=0.2$ (Supplementary Table S6): stratified methods restored worst-stratum coverage to {{T:T5_alpha_sensitivity|alpha=0.05&method=SDLC|worst_stratum|f3}} and {{T:T5_alpha_sensitivity|alpha=0.2&method=SDLC|worst_stratum|f3}}, and SDLC had the smallest stratified sets. Finer strata protected narrower ranges of the target: with decile strata (S10) the worst decile coverage of SDLC rose to {{T:T6_strata_schemes|scheme=S10&method=SDLC|worst_decile|f3}}, from {{T:T6_strata_schemes|scheme=S5&method=SDLC|worst_decile|f3}} with five strata, at a size increase from {{T:T6_strata_schemes|scheme=S5&method=SDLC|size_geo|f3}} to {{T:T6_strata_schemes|scheme=S10&method=SDLC|size_geo|f3}}. The floor variant (SDLC-floor, $\beta=0.2$) reached its weaker guarantee with smaller sets (worst stratum {{T:T5_alpha_sensitivity|alpha=0.1&method=SDLC-floor|worst_stratum|f3}}, size {{T:T5_alpha_sensitivity|alpha=0.1&method=SDLC-floor|size_geo|f3}}).

*Calibration-set size.* Stratified calibration needs enough calibration points in every stratum (Supplementary Fig. S3, Table S8). With a quarter of the calibration split (on average {{T:T7_calibration_size|cal_frac=0.25&method=SDLC|mean_n_cal|int}} points) stratum-wise validity was retained, but the stratified sets grew from {{T:T7_calibration_size|cal_frac=1.0&method=SDLC|size_geo|f3}} to {{T:T7_calibration_size|cal_frac=0.25&method=SDLC|size_geo|f3}} for SDLC. The main reason was that a tail stratum with fewer than $1/\alpha-1$ points has an infinite threshold, which happened in {{T:T7_calibration_size|cal_frac=0.25&method=SDLC|runs_with_inf|int}} runs, against {{T:T7_calibration_size|cal_frac=1.0&method=SDLC|runs_with_inf|int}} with the full split. Marginal methods are insensitive to this, and for small datasets stratified calibration should be combined with fewer strata or a lower stratum level $\beta$ (the floor variant).

*Density estimator.* Among the density estimators, the symmetric location–scale density was as efficient as the two-piece variant and much more efficient than the histogram density (Table [-@tbl:size]); the defensive $t$ component and the cross-fitted standardisation are part of every result reported here.

*Base learner.* With a two-layer perceptron as point and scale model (same pipeline, {{mlp.n_seeds}} seeds), the coverage results were unchanged: marginal methods reached a worst-stratum coverage of {{T:T9_mlp_summary|method=SCP|worst_stratum|f3}}–{{T:T9_mlp_summary|method=DCP|worst_stratum|f3}}, stratified methods {{T:T9_mlp_summary|method=BCCP|worst_stratum|f3}}–{{T:T9_mlp_summary|method=SDLC|worst_stratum|f3}}. SDLC again gave the smallest stratified sets: smaller than BCCP on {{mlp.BCCP.wins}} of {{mlp.n_datasets}} datasets (ratio {{mlp.BCCP.geo}}, Holm $p$ = {{mlp.BCCP.p}}), than S-NCP on {{mlp.S-NCP.wins}} (ratio {{mlp.S-NCP.geo}}, $p$ {{mlp.S-NCP.p}}) and than S-HPD on {{mlp.S-HPD.wins}} (ratio {{mlp.S-HPD.geo}}, $p$ {{mlp.S-HPD.p}}). The gain over S-NCP was larger than with gradient boosting.

## Error analysis {#sec:error}

Where does stratification cost the most, and where does SDLC lose to simpler scores? The price of stratum-wise validity (SDLC relative to DLS) rose with the skewness of the target (Spearman $\rho$ = {{ea.spearman_price_abs_skew.rho}}, $p$ {{ea.spearman_price_abs_skew.p}}) and fell with the calibration-set size ($\rho$ = {{ea.spearman_price_log_ncal.rho}}, $p$ = {{ea.spearman_price_log_ncal.p}}); it was weakly related to the predictability of the target ($\rho$ = {{ea.spearman_price_rmse.rho}}, $p$ = {{ea.spearman_price_rmse.p}}). The largest price occurred for forest fires ({{T:T10_error_analysis|dataset=forest_fires|price|f2}}): the burned area is zero for {{J:metrics_v3.json|forest_zero_frac|pct}} of the fires, the atom at zero merges the two lower strata, and covering the heavy upper tail of an almost unpredictable target requires very long sets. SDLC was larger than S-NCP on {{h3.S-NCP.losses}} datasets: the social-mobility table (socmob, ratio {{T:T10_error_analysis|dataset=socmob|vs_sncp|f2}}), cars ({{T:T10_error_analysis|dataset=cars|vs_sncp|f2}}), forest fires ({{T:T10_error_analysis|dataset=forest_fires|vs_sncp|f2}}) and QSAR fish toxicity ({{T:T10_error_analysis|dataset=QSAR_fish_toxicity|vs_sncp|f2}}). All four are small or have many ties, so that the scale and noise models are estimated from few distinct values. The ratio of SDLC to S-NCP was correlated with the relative RMSE of the point model ($\rho$ = {{ea.spearman_vs_sncp_rmse.rho}}, $p$ = {{ea.spearman_vs_sncp_rmse.p}}): the density score helps most when the location model is accurate and the noise is heteroscedastic. Supplementary Fig. S2 relates the worst-stratum coverage of each dataset to its null reference; the lowest values occur on the smallest calibration sets and remain within the range expected under exact validity.
