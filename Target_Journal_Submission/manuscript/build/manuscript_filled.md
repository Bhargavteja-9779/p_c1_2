# 1. Introduction

Many regression problems that intelligent systems are asked to solve are judged by how they handle the *rare* outcomes: the unusually expensive property, the exceptional salary, the extreme load on a power grid, the very toxic compound. These outcomes are rare by definition, so they are under-represented in training data, and models trained by minimising average loss shrink their predictions towards the bulk of the target distribution. This is the setting of *imbalanced regression* [@branco2016survey; @ribeiro2020imbalanced; @yang2021delving], where a growing literature re-weights, re-samples or re-parameterises models so that point predictions for rare targets become more accurate.

Point predictions are only half of what a decision maker needs. Prediction *sets* (or intervals) with a coverage guarantee state how far the truth may be from the prediction, and conformal prediction delivers such guarantees in finite samples for any model, under exchangeability only [@vovk2005alrw; @lei2018distribution; @angelopoulos2023gentle]. Its standard guarantee, however, is *marginal*: the set contains the outcome with probability $1-\alpha$ *on average over all outcomes*. When rare outcomes are what matter, the relevant question is different: **when the outcome is extreme, how often does the set contain it?** A marginal guarantee is silent on this question, because the frequent bulk of the distribution can be over-covered while the rare tails are under-covered and the average still equals $1-\alpha$.

A large body of work makes conformal sets adapt to the covariates, so that coverage holds approximately conditionally on $X$ [@romano2019conformalized; @chernozhukov2021distributional; @izbicki2022cdsplit; @gibbs2025conformal]. One might expect such sets also to protect rare outcomes. They do not, and better estimation would not change this: even the *oracle* set with exact coverage $1-\alpha$ at every $x$ under-covers the tails of the target, because an extreme outcome typically combines a large signal with a large, unpredictable noise realisation that no $X$-measurable set can anticipate. Coverage *conditional on the target*, which we call stratum-wise coverage, is a different property that must be targeted explicitly.

Calibrating conformal thresholds separately within bins of the observed outcome is the natural device for this purpose, and it is not new. @randahl2026bin proposed it as bin-conditional conformal prediction (BCCP) for the heavily skewed distribution of conflict fatalities, and @wang2023equal calibrate within bins of the outcome to obtain equal coverage across groups. Its finite-sample bin-wise validity is an instance of Mondrian (label-conditional) validity [@vovk2005alrw; @vovk2013conditional]. Two questions remain open for regression. First, the guarantee holds for any score, so efficiency is decided by the score, and it is not known which score makes stratum-valid sets smallest or what that efficiency costs. Second, strata protect rare outcomes only at their own resolution: the most extreme outcomes lie inside a tail stratum and are covered only on average with it, while finer strata leave too few calibration points. Imbalanced regression usually describes the importance of an outcome not by strata but by a continuous *relevance function* [@torgo2007utility; @ribeiro2020imbalanced; @steininger2021denseweight], and long-tailed classification has recently obtained conformal guarantees that average coverage over classes [@ding2025longtailed; @bhattacharyya2026macro]; a regression counterpart that weights coverage by relevance has been missing. This paper makes four contributions.

1. **Two guarantees for rare outcomes and their optimal sets.** We quantify how far the oracle covariate-conditional set falls short in the tails (Proposition 1: 70.5% of the top decile in a Gaussian model with half of the variance explained). For coverage within strata, conditional-density level sets with stratum-specific thresholds are the smallest valid sets (Proposition 2, the per-stratum form of known level-set results [@lei2013distribution; @sadinle2019least]), and their plug-in version is asymptotically optimal under conditions on the density estimate (Proposition 3). For *relevance-weighted coverage*, the average of coverage weighted by a relevance function, we prove finite-sample validity of relevance-weighted calibration (Theorem 2) and show that level sets of relevance times conditional density are the smallest valid sets (Proposition 4).
2. **Two methods with one density model.** Stratified density-level conformal prediction (SDLC) and the new relevance-weighted density-level conformal prediction (RW-DLC) share a semiparametric location–scale density whose noise distribution is estimated from cross-fitted residuals; both have exact implementations whose cost is linear in the number of test points.
3. **Evidence under pre-specified plans.** On all 31 eligible OpenML-CTR23 datasets with 10 predefined splits, we compare the stratified methods with BCCP, stratified normalised-residual, quantile and HPD scores and seven marginal or covariate-adaptive methods, with ablations of each score component. The size comparisons are replicated on 17 held-out datasets under an analysis plan fixed before evaluation, and RW-DLC is evaluated on both collections under a second plan fixed before its first evaluation. We report where the gains are small: among scores that use the estimated scale, the choice changes set sizes by only a few per cent.
4. **Decision-oriented guidance.** We compare tail alerts derived from prediction sets with dedicated conformal tail classifiers and conformal selection [@jin2023selection] under explicit cost models, analyse robustness to target shift, imbalance-aware training, a second learner family, grouped splits, the miscoverage level, the strata and the calibration-set size, and state which guarantee and which score suit which requirement.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews related work and positions the contributions (Table 1). Section 3 formalises the problem and analyses the oracle covariate-conditional set. Section 4 presents stratified calibration, the optimal score and SDLC, and introduces relevance-weighted coverage and RW-DLC. Section 5 describes the experimental design, Section 6 reports the results, Section 7 discusses implications and limitations, and Section 8 concludes.


# 2. Related work

Four lines of work meet in this paper: conformal regression with covariate-adaptive coverage, calibration within categories defined by the label, imbalanced regression, and conformal prediction under distribution shift.

## 2.1 Conformal regression and covariate-conditional coverage

Split (inductive) conformal prediction calibrates a nonconformity score of an arbitrary fitted regressor on held-out data and guarantees marginal coverage in finite samples under exchangeability [@papadopoulos2002inductive; @vovk2005alrw; @lei2018distribution]. Much subsequent work changes the score so that the sets adapt to the covariates. Locally weighted (normalised) conformal prediction divides absolute residuals by an estimate of their spread [@lei2018distribution]; conformalised quantile regression (CQR) calibrates the interval between two estimated conditional quantiles [@romano2019conformalized; @sesia2020comparison]; and distributional conformal prediction (DCP) uses the probability integral transform of an estimated conditional distribution function [@chernozhukov2021distributional]. Conformal predictive distributions [@vovk2019nonparametric] and jackknife+/CV+ intervals, which reuse all data at higher computational cost [@barber2021jackknife], are further alternatives. A family of methods builds sets from density estimates: density level sets, conformalised without covariates and asymptotically optimal [@lei2013distribution] and extended to regression by local conformal bands [@lei2014bands]; Dist-split and CD-split [@izbicki2020flexible]; HPD-split, which conformalises highest-density regions of an estimated conditional density [@izbicki2022cdsplit]; conformal sets from conditional histograms, which adapt to skewness [@sesia2021conformal]; regression-as-classification, which learns a density over a discretised target [@guha2024conformal]; and conformal thresholded intervals, which threshold the estimated conditional quantiles to shorten the sets [@luo2025cti]. Under coverage constraints indexed by a finite-dimensional class of covariate shifts, length-optimal sets are level sets of the conditional density with a covariate-dependent level [@kiyani2024length]. SDLC uses a score from this family but calibrates it with stratum-specific thresholds.

These methods target approximate covariate-conditional coverage, $P\{Y\in C(X)\,|\, X=x\}\approx 1-\alpha$, which cannot be guaranteed in finite samples without further assumptions [@barber2021limits]. The literature has therefore pursued relaxed guarantees and approximations: local conformal bands with asymptotic conditional validity [@lei2014bands]; conditional calibration, which guarantees coverage over a user-specified class of covariate groups or shifts [@gibbs2025conformal]; localised calibration, which weights calibration points by their proximity to the test point in covariate space [@guan2023localized; @hore2025local]; calibration within the cells of covariate partitions learned by regression trees [@cabezas2025regression] or by the leaf paths of gradient-boosted trees [@santos2026loboost]; and learned scores or adjusted regressors that reduce conditional miscoverage [@xie2024boosted; @gao2024adjusting]. In applied intelligent systems, conformal prediction serves as an uncertainty layer, for example for option prices [@bastos2024conformal], tropical-cyclone track forecasts [@meng2024uncertainty] and the carbonation depth of recycled-aggregate concrete [@hajibabaee2025carbonation]. All of these approaches are marginal or condition, exactly or approximately, on functions of $X$, and Proposition 1 (Section 3) shows that, in a Gaussian model, even exact covariate-conditional coverage leaves extreme outcomes under-covered. Covariate-adaptive scores are therefore complementary to stratified calibration rather than a substitute for it.

## 2.2 Category-, label- and bin-conditional calibration

Mondrian conformal predictors [@vovk2005alrw; @vovk2022alrw] assign each example to a category through a taxonomy that may depend on both the object and its label, and calibrate separately within each category; this yields finite-sample coverage conditional on the category [@angelopoulos2024theoretical]. @vovk2013conditional distinguished object-, label- and training-conditional validity and showed that Mondrian inductive conformal predictors achieve category-conditional, and in particular label-conditional, validity; @toccaceli2019combination studied label-conditional inductive Mondrian predictors and their combination.

In classification, the corresponding property is class-conditional coverage. When classes are many and calibration examples per class are few, clustered conformal prediction pools classes with similar score distributions [@ding2023class], a device analogous to merging sparse target strata; BCOPS constructs label-conditional sets from density estimates [@guan2022prediction]; Mondrian conformal prediction has been applied to product verification [@oucheikh2022product], and conformal uncertainty measures have been built into deep forests for classification [@zhang2025conformal]. Closest to our optimality result is work on set-valued classifiers with class-specific error control [@lei2014classification], in particular @sadinle2019least, who showed that the least ambiguous classifier under class-specific coverage thresholds the conditional class probabilities $p(y\,|\, x)$ with class-specific cut-offs. Proposition 2 is the same characterisation for a continuous outcome, with Lebesgue measure as the size and target strata in place of classes; unified treatments of such set-valued classifiers are surveyed by @chzhen2021setvalued. For long-tailed classification, where many classes are rare, @ding2025longtailed propose a prevalence-adjusted score and interpolate between marginal and class-conditional thresholds, @liu2025longtail balance marginal and class-conditional coverage, and @bhattacharyya2026macro show that label-weighted conformal prediction guarantees macro-coverage, the unweighted average of per-class coverage, and characterise the smallest sets that achieve it. RW-DLC carries the last idea to continuous outcomes, with a relevance function in place of class weights (Section 4.7). Group-conditional coverage is also the basis of equalized coverage across protected groups in fair prediction [@romano2020malice]. Kandinsky conformal prediction [@bairaktari2025kandinsky] provides conditional coverage for overlapping or fractional groups defined jointly on covariates and labels, includes regression, and contains covariate-group-conditional, class-conditional and Mondrian conformal prediction as special cases, with a high-probability guarantee whose slack grows with the complexity of the group class. Disjoint target strata are its simplest label-defined groups, for which Mondrian calibration gives an exact finite-sample guarantee; overlapping groups (for example strata and deciles together) and weighted groups (for example tilts of the target distribution) are a natural way to refine the guarantee beyond a single partition.

In regression, Mondrian categories have mostly been defined through the covariates or the fitted model: by binning difficulty estimates [@bostrom2020mondrian], with interpretable regression trees [@johansson2018interpretable], by clusters of the covariates [@sousa2024improving] and by quartiles of out-of-fold point predictions [@boruah2026distribution]; Mondrian taxonomies have also been applied to conformal predictive distributions [@bostrom2021mondrian]. The last study reports that split conformal intervals for steel fatigue strength, although marginally valid, under-cover the highest-strength quartile; a follow-up shows that worst-bin coverage statistics are biased downwards and proposes a null correction [@boruah2026null]. Because the categories are functions of $X$, the resulting guarantees do not extend to coverage conditional on $Y$.

As of November 2026, we are aware of two regression methods that calibrate within bins of the outcome itself. @wang2023equal define equal opportunity of coverage, which requires coverage to be equal across groups of a sensitive attribute conditionally on the true outcome; their binned fair quantile regression (BFQR) splits $Y$ into equal-mass bins and calibrates within each combination of group and bin, with a conformal step that maintains coverage, and they illustrate that standard methods mis-cover the head and tail bins of the outcome. Their aim is fairness, but the binning device is the same. BFQR forms equal-mass bins of the calibration outcomes, takes within each group and bin the $\beta_m$-quantile of conformalised quantile-regression scores, and chooses the bin levels $\beta_m$ to minimise the average width subject to $\sum_m\beta_m/M=1-\alpha$; their Theorem 4.1 shows that coverage in each group and bin lies between $\beta_m$ and $\beta_m+1/(n_{a,m}+1)$. Coverage is thus equalised across groups within a bin but deliberately traded across bins. The most closely related work is the bin-conditional conformal prediction (BCCP) of @randahl2026bin, motivated by the heavily skewed distribution of fatalities from armed conflict. BCCP partitions the calibration scores by user-defined bins of the observed outcome, runs split conformal prediction with a residual score within each bin over a grid of candidate outcomes, and returns either the union of the per-bin sets, which may be discontiguous, or its hull ("contiguized" sets). The authors state that BCCP guarantees coverage within each bin under within-bin exchangeability, describe it as a hybrid of label-conditional (Mondrian) and split conformal prediction, and evaluate it on simulations and on conflict fatalities.

The stratified calibration step of the present paper (Section 4, Eqs. 3–4) coincides with BCCP: with the absolute-residual score and the strata as bins it is BCCP, and taking the convex hull, as in our hull variant, gives its contiguized form. Its finite-sample validity (Theorem 1) is an instance of Mondrian validity and is not claimed as new, and its argument does not depend on the score. BCCP leaves open which score yields the smallest bin-valid sets, how the score's efficiency comes about, and how the guarantee behaves under label shift. The present paper addresses these questions: the oracle analysis of covariate-conditional sets (Proposition 1), the size-optimal score under per-stratum coverage and the asymptotic optimality of its plug-in version (Propositions 2 and 3), the SDLC procedure built on them, the label-shift analysis (Corollary 1), and large-scale evidence in which BCCP, stratified versions of the normalised-residual, quantile and HPD scores, and marginal and covariate-adaptive methods serve as baselines.

## 2.3 Imbalanced regression and its uncertainty

Imbalanced regression concerns problems in which the target values that matter most are rare in the training data [@branco2016survey; @ribeiro2020imbalanced]. Utility-based regression encodes the non-uniform importance of targets through relevance and utility functions [@torgo2007utility], and precision and recall for regression [@torgo2009precision] and the SERA metric [@ribeiro2020imbalanced] evaluate point predictions on the relevant, typically extreme, part of the target range. Data-level methods re-balance the training set: SMOTER [@torgo2013smote] and SMOGN, which adds Gaussian noise [@branco2017smogn], synthesise examples with rare targets, and @avelino2024resampling survey and benchmark such re-sampling strategies. Recent contributions include oversampling with nearest neighbours and autoencoders [@belhaouari2024oversampling], the refinement of oversampled examples by a generative adversarial network [@alahyari2026smogan], a hybrid of data-level and algorithm-level balancing [@shahbazi2026hybrid] and soft-computing techniques that actively address imbalance [@arteaga2023enhancing]. Algorithm-level methods re-weight the loss: DenseWeight weights training examples inversely to the estimated density of their target [@steininger2021denseweight], label and feature distribution smoothing exploit the similarity of nearby targets in deep models [@yang2021delving], and Balanced MSE corrects the regression loss for the imbalance of the training label distribution [@ren2022balanced].

Uncertainty has entered this literature mainly as a means to better point predictions or as a by-product of probabilistic modelling. Quantile-regression ensembles report prediction intervals for small-sample, long-tailed targets without a finite-sample coverage guarantee [@zhu2026swarm]. Variational imbalanced regression obtains uncertainty estimates through probabilistic smoothing [@wang2023variational], and UQDIR identifies rare samples through epistemic uncertainty and re-samples them with a weight function to improve accuracy [@dolar2025uncertainty]; neither provides a coverage guarantee. Extreme conformal prediction addresses a different difficulty, very high confidence levels, by combining extreme-value theory with conformal prediction [@pasche2026extreme]. Guarantees on how often prediction sets contain the rare targets themselves, the uncertainty counterpart of the relevance-based evaluation above, have so far come from conflict forecasting and fair regression [@randahl2026bin; @wang2023equal] rather than from the imbalanced-regression literature (Section 2.2). Where the decision is only whether a unit's outcome is extreme, dedicated procedures exist: conformal selection flags units whose outcome exceeds a threshold with control of the false discovery rate [@jin2023selection], and a class-conditional conformal classifier on the event "outcome in the tail" controls the miss rate. We use both as baselines for tail alerts (Section 6).

## 2.4 Distribution shift

Weighted conformal prediction restores validity under covariate shift by re-weighting calibration scores with the likelihood ratio of the covariate distributions [@tibshirani2019conformal]. Under label shift, also called target shift, the marginal distribution of $Y$ changes while the conditional distribution of $X$ given $Y$ does not. For classification, @podkopaev2021distribution study conformal prediction under label shift, using re-weighted and label-conditional calibration. For regression, @yi2025distribution identify the likelihood ratio under target shift and construct highest-density sets from a weight-adjusted conditional density, and @lee2026split combine a label-shift-adjusted Bayesian score with importance weighting. The re-weighting approaches estimate the shift [@yi2025distribution; @lee2026split], and @choi2026continuous analyse the sensitivity of conformal Bayes sets to misspecified exponential tilts of the response, showing that exact validity is lost when the tilt is unknown; label-conditional calibration in classification needs no estimate, because a label shift re-weights whole classes [@podkopaev2021distribution].

Stratified calibration carries this property over to regression: it needs no estimate of the shift, because a guarantee that holds within every stratum is unaffected by changes in the stratum probabilities. This invariance is exact when the shift re-weights whole strata, that is, when the conditional distribution of $(X,Y)$ within each stratum is unchanged (Corollary 1). If the density ratio varies inside a stratum, the within-stratum distribution changes and the guarantee holds only approximately, which our experiments quantify under target tilting (Section 5).

# 3. Preliminaries and problem formulation

## 3.1 Target-conditional coverage

Let $(X,Y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}$ have joint law $P$, with marginal law $P_X$ of the covariates. A *prediction set* is a map $C$ from $\mathcal{X}$ to subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ whose graph $\{(x,y):y\in C(x)\}$ is jointly measurable. Split conformal prediction [@papadopoulos2002inductive; @lei2018distribution] guarantees *marginal* coverage $P\{Y\in C(X)\}\ge 1-\alpha$, and much recent work targets approximate *covariate-conditional* coverage $P\{Y\in C(X)\,|\, X=x\}\approx 1-\alpha$ [@romano2019conformalized; @izbicki2022cdsplit; @gibbs2025conformal], which cannot be guaranteed in finite samples without further assumptions [@barber2021limits].

In imbalanced regression the outcomes of interest are rare target values. Let $e_1<\dots<e_{K-1}$ be cut points and $B_1=(-\infty,e_1)$, $B_k=[e_{k-1},e_k)$ for $1<k<K$, $B_K=[e_{K-1},\infty)$ the resulting *target strata*, for instance the lower tail, the bulk and the upper tail, with $p_k=P(Y\in B_k)>0$. We call
$$\operatorname{Cov}_k(C)=P\{Y\in C(X)\,|\, Y\in B_k\},\qquad k=1,\ldots,K, \qquad (1)$$
the *stratum-wise* (target-conditional) coverage of $C$. It answers the question that matters when extremes are costly: *when the outcome is extreme, how often does the prediction set contain it?* It is the regression analogue of class-conditional coverage in classification [@vovk2005alrw; @vovk2013conditional; @sadinle2019least] and the outcome-conditional notion behind equal opportunity of coverage [@wang2023equal] and bin-conditional conformal prediction [@randahl2026bin]. Because $\sum_k p_k\,\operatorname{Cov}_k(C)=P\{Y\in C(X)\}$, marginal coverage only constrains a $p$-weighted average of the $\operatorname{Cov}_k$. In particular, a set with *exact* coverage $1-\alpha$, marginally or at every $x$, has $\min_k\operatorname{Cov}_k(C)\le 1-\alpha$, with equality only if all strata are covered at exactly the same rate; any over-coverage of the bulk is paid for in some other stratum.

## 3.2 Covariate-conditional validity does not imply target-conditional validity

How large can the shortfall be for the best covariate-conditional set? The following proposition answers this in the simplest model.

**Proposition 1 (Gaussian model).** *Let $X\sim N(0,\tau^2)$ and $Y\,|\, X\sim N(X,\sigma^2)$ with $\tau^2+\sigma^2=1$ and $0<\tau^2<1$, so that $R^2=\tau^2$ is the fraction of variance explained by the optimal predictor. Let $z=\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)$ and $C^{\mathrm{cov}}(x)=[x-z\sigma,\,x+z\sigma]$, which is, up to Lebesgue-null sets, the unique set of minimal length with $P\{Y\in C(x)\,|\, X=x\}\ge 1-\alpha$ for every $x$. Then*
$$P\{Y\in C^{\mathrm{cov}}(X)\,|\, Y=y\}=\Phi\!\left(\frac{z\sigma-(1-R^2)y}{s}\right)-\Phi\!\left(\frac{-z\sigma-(1-R^2)y}{s}\right),\qquad s=\sigma\sqrt{R^2}, \qquad (2)$$
*an even function of $y$ that is strictly decreasing in $|y|$ and tends to $0$ as $|y|\to\infty$.*

*Proof.* $(X,Y)$ is bivariate normal with $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=1$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\tau^2$, hence $X\,|\, Y=y\sim N(\tau^2y,\tau^2\sigma^2)$ and $Y-X\,|\, Y=y\sim N((1-\tau^2)y,\tau^2\sigma^2)$. Since $Y\in C^{\mathrm{cov}}(X)$ iff $|Y-X|\le z\sigma$, (2) follows. Write $a=z\sigma>0$ and $m=(1-R^2)y$. The function $h(m)=\Phi((a-m)/s)-\Phi((-a-m)/s)$ is even because $\Phi(-u)=1-\Phi(u)$, and for $m>0$, $h'(m)=-\{\phi((a-m)/s)-\phi((a+m)/s)\}/s<0$ because $|a-m|<a+m$. As $1-R^2>0$, (2) is strictly decreasing in $|y|$ and vanishes as $|y|\to\infty$. Minimality and uniqueness of $C^{\mathrm{cov}}(x)$ follow because the $N(x,\sigma^2)$ density is symmetric and strictly unimodal. $\square$

Integrating (2) over strata of $Y$ (Table 2): for a predictor that explains half of the variance, the oracle covariate-conditional interval covers 70.5% of the top-10% outcomes and 37.5% of the top-1% outcomes, against 94.9% of the central 80%; with $R^2=0.9$ it covers 86.2% of the top decile. Proposition 1 is specific to the Gaussian model, and the shape of the coverage profile is not universal. Table 2 therefore also reports Monte Carlo values for heavy-tailed signals and for skewed noise (oracle $X$-conditional shortest sets; details in the table note). With a Student-$t$ signal and Gaussian noise the tail strata are still under-covered at $R^2=0.5$ (top 10%: 0.648 for $t_3$), but coverage no longer vanishes in the extreme tail: for $t_3$ the top 1% is covered at 0.625, because extreme outcomes are then mostly produced by extreme signals, which a covariate-conditional set follows. With right-skewed Gamma noise the oracle *over*-covers the lower decile (0.987) and covers only 0.418 of the top decile and 0.029 of the top 1%. In every case at least one tail stratum is covered well below $1-\alpha$. Tail under-coverage of standard conformal sets has been reported empirically [@wang2023equal; @randahl2026bin; @boruah2026distribution]; Proposition 1 and its extensions show that it persists for the oracle, so better covariate adaptivity cannot remove it. To guarantee coverage in rare strata, a set must be calibrated against the distribution of $(X,Y)$ *given* $Y\in B_k$, which is what stratified calibration does.

# 4. Proposed method: stratified density-level conformal prediction

## 4.1 Stratified (Mondrian) calibration

Data are split into a proper training set $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and a calibration set $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}=\{(X_i,Y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$. Using $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ only, we fit a nonconformity score $s:\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}$ (small values = conforming) and choose the cut points, e.g. at quantiles of the training targets. Let $k(y)$ be the stratum containing $y$, $I_k=\{i:Y_i\in B_k\}$, $n_k=|I_k|$, and, for levels $\alpha_k\in(0,1)$,
$$\hat q_k=\text{the }r_k\text{-th smallest value of }\{s(X_i,Y_i):i\in I_k\},\qquad r_k=\lceil (n_k+1)(1-\alpha_k)\rceil, \qquad (3)$$
with $\hat q_k=+\infty$ if $r_k>n_k$, which happens exactly when $n_k<1/\alpha_k-1$ (i.e. $n_k\le 8$ for $\alpha_k=0.1$). The stratified prediction set is
$$\hat C(x)=\{y\in\mathbb{R}: s(x,y)\le \hat q_{k(y)}\}=\bigcup_{k=1}^K\{y\in B_k: s(x,y)\le\hat q_k\}. \qquad (4)$$
The threshold applied to a candidate value $y$ is that of the stratum *of that candidate*, not of a prediction; this is what makes the guarantee target-conditional. Equation (4) is an inductive Mondrian conformal predictor whose taxonomy assigns each example to the stratum of its label [@vovk2005alrw; @vovk2013conditional; @toccaceli2019combination], and it is not new for regression. With the absolute-residual score it is the bin-conditional conformal prediction (BCCP) of @randahl2026bin, who report either the union (4) or its convex hull and note that the bin-wise guarantee holds for any score. @wang2023equal calibrate quantile-regression scores within equal-mass bins of $Y$ and groups of a sensitive attribute, at bin-specific levels that are optimised for width and average to $1-\alpha$, and prove the corresponding group-and-bin-conditional guarantee. Kandinsky conformal prediction [@bairaktari2025kandinsky] covers far richer families: overlapping or fractional groups defined jointly on covariates and labels, with a high-probability guarantee whose slack grows with the dimension of the group class. Disjoint target strata are its simplest special case, for which (4) gives the exact finite-sample guarantee below. When every sub-level set $\{y:s(x,y)\le q\}$ is an interval, as for residual or quantile scores, $\hat C(x)$ is a union of at most $K$ intervals; density scores with a multimodal estimate can produce more pieces.

The following guarantee is the standard label-conditional Mondrian property [@vovk2005alrw; @vovk2013conditional]; for bins of a continuous outcome it is stated by @wang2023equal [Thm. 4.1] and @randahl2026bin. We restate it for an arbitrary score, with the edge cases used later, and give the proof in Appendix A for completeness.

**Theorem 1 (stratum-wise validity of Mondrian calibration).** *Suppose that, conditionally on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$, the points $(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,(X_n,Y_n),(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})$ are exchangeable, and let $p_k=P(Y_{n+1}\in B_k\,|\,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}})$. Then for every $k$ with $p_k>0$,*
$$P\{Y_{n+1}\in\hat C(X_{n+1})\,|\, Y_{n+1}\in B_k,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}\}\ \ge\ 1-\alpha_k, \qquad (5)$$
*and consequently $P\{Y_{n+1}\in\hat C(X_{n+1})\,|\,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}\}\ge 1-\sum_kp_k\alpha_k\ge 1-\max_k\alpha_k$. If the scores within stratum $k$ are almost surely distinct and finite, then also $P\{Y_{n+1}\in\hat C(X_{n+1})\,|\, Y_{n+1}\in B_k,n_k,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}\}\le 1-\alpha_k+1/(n_k+1)$. The levels $\alpha_k$ may depend on $n_k$, and the score and strata may be any functions of $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$.*

The guarantee holds for *any* score. A consequence concerns shifts of the target distribution between calibration and deployment; it collects known facts, specialised to target strata.

**Corollary 1 (target shift).** *Let $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$ be i.i.d. from $P$ and let the test point be drawn from $Q$, independently of $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$ and $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$. Write $P_k=P(\cdot\,|\, Y\in B_k)$ and $Q_k=Q(\cdot\,|\, Y\in B_k)$ for the within-stratum laws of the pair $(X,Y)$ and $\pi_k=Q(Y\in B_k)$; assume $p_k>0$ whenever $\pi_k>0$.*
*(i) If $Q_k=P_k$ for every $k$ with $\pi_k>0$ (the shift re-weights whole strata), then $Q\{Y\in\hat C(X)\,|\, Y\in B_k\}\ge 1-\alpha_k$ for every such $k$, and $Q\{Y\in\hat C(X)\}\ge 1-\sum_k\pi_k\alpha_k$ whatever the $\pi_k$.*
*(ii) For an arbitrary shift, $Q\{Y\in\hat C(X)\,|\, Y\in B_k\}\ge 1-\alpha_k-d_{\operatorname{TV}}(Q_k,P_k)$.*
*(iii) Under label shift, $Q(\cdot\,|\, Y=y)=P(\cdot\,|\, Y=y)$ with density ratio $w=dQ_Y/dP_Y$; let $m_k$ and $M_k$ be the essential infimum and supremum of $w$ on $B_k$, $0<m_k\le M_k<\infty$. Then $Q\{Y\notin\hat C(X)\,|\, Y\in B_k\}\le(M_k/m_k)\,\alpha_k$ and $d_{\operatorname{TV}}(Q_k,P_k)\le\tanh\{\log(M_k/m_k)/4\}$.*

The proof is in Appendix A. Part (i) is the continuous-outcome form of the label-shift robustness of label-conditional coverage in classification [@podkopaev2021distribution; @angelopoulos2024theoretical]. The point specific to a continuous outcome is that a label shift generally also changes the distribution of $Y$ *within* a stratum; exactness then requires that the joint law of $(X,Y)$ inside each stratum be unchanged, and a stable covariate distribution alone is not sufficient. Part (ii) is the total-variation form of the coverage-gap bounds of @barber2023beyond [Sec. 4.5], applied within a stratum, and part (iii) is the standard consequence of a bounded likelihood ratio. Both bounds are loose for the shifts studied in Experiment 4, whose density ratios are unbounded on the open tail strata, so they explain the direction of the effect rather than its size: degradation is small when the density ratio varies little across each stratum, which favours narrow strata at the price of fewer calibration points per stratum. Marginally calibrated sets enjoy no analogue of (i): their coverage under $Q$ is $\sum_k\pi_k\operatorname{Cov}_k$, which can be as low as $\min_k\operatorname{Cov}_k$. Unlike re-weighting approaches [@podkopaev2021distribution; @yi2025distribution; @lee2026split; for covariate shift @tibshirani2019conformal], stratified calibration needs no estimate of the shift, but it is exact only for shifts that act on the stratum probabilities.

## 4.2 The smallest sets with stratum-wise coverage

Theorem 1 leaves the score free, so validity is never the issue; the score determines how large the sets are. For a prediction set $C$ let $|C(x)|$ be its Lebesgue measure and $\mathbb{E}|C(X)|=\int|C(x)|\,P_X(dx)$ its expected size. The size-optimal sets are density level sets, by the Neyman–Pearson argument that underlies conformal density level sets [@lei2013distribution], least-ambiguous set-valued classifiers with class-specific coverage [@sadinle2019least; @lei2014classification] and, for coverage constraints indexed by groups or shifts of the covariates, the length-optimal sets of @kiyani2024length, whose level depends on $x$. The constraints in (1) are indexed by the *label* instead, so the problem separates by stratum and the solution has thresholds that are constant within each stratum. We state the result for this case because it identifies the score that stratified calibration should use.

**Proposition 2 (size-optimal stratum-wise sets).** *Suppose the conditional law of $Y$ given $X$ has a Lebesgue density $f(y\,|\, x)$, that $f(Y\,|\, X)>0$ almost surely, and that for each $k$ the random variable $f(Y\,|\, X)$ has a continuous distribution conditionally on $Y\in B_k$. Fix $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_K\in(0,1)$. Then there are thresholds $t_k>0$ with $P\{f(Y\,|\, X)\ge t_k\,|\, Y\in B_k\}=1-\alpha_k$, and the set*
$$C^\star(x)=\bigcup_{k=1}^K\{y\in B_k: f(y\,|\, x)\ge t_k\} \qquad (6)$$
*satisfies $\operatorname{Cov}_k(C^\star)=1-\alpha_k$ for all $k$. Every prediction set $C$ with $\operatorname{Cov}_k(C)\ge 1-\alpha_k$ for all $k$ satisfies $\mathbb{E}|C(X)\cap B_k|\ge\mathbb{E}|C^\star(X)\cap B_k|$ for every $k$, and hence $\mathbb{E}|C(X)|\ge\mathbb{E}|C^\star(X)|$.*

The proof (Appendix A) is the per-stratum Neyman–Pearson argument. Since the inequality holds for every realisation of a set built from data independent of the test point, $C^\star$ also minimises expected size among data-dependent (e.g. conformal) sets whose stratum-wise coverage, averaged over the data, is at least $1-\alpha_k$. With $K=1$ it reduces to a single-threshold conditional-density level set; HPD-split [@izbicki2022cdsplit] instead uses an $x$-specific threshold, whose oracle is the highest-density region of each conditional distribution. Proposition 2 is an oracle statement. The next result shows that stratified calibration of a consistent density estimate attains it asymptotically (strata estimated from training quantiles converge to fixed cut points, which we take as given); its proof (Appendix A) follows the plug-in arguments of @lei2013distribution and @izbicki2022cdsplit.

**Proposition 3 (asymptotic size-optimality of the plug-in procedure).** *Let the assumptions of Proposition 2 hold, and suppose in addition that the distribution function of $f(Y\,|\, X)$ given $Y\in B_k$ is strictly increasing at $t_k$ for every $k$. Let $\hat f$ be estimated from $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ of size $N$, let $\varepsilon_N=\sup_{x,y}|\hat f(y\,|\, x)-f(y\,|\, x)|\to0$ in probability, and let $\hat C$ be the stratified set (3)–(4) with score $s=-\hat f$, fixed strata $B_1,\ldots,B_K$ and $n\to\infty$ calibration points. Then $-\hat q_k\to t_k$ in probability for every $k$, and*
$$\mathbb{E}_X\big|\hat C(X)\,\triangle\,C^\star(X)\big|\ \to\ 0\quad\text{in probability,} \qquad (7)$$
*where $\mathbb{E}_X$ averages over a new $X\sim P_X$ given the data. In particular $\mathbb{E}_X|\hat C(X)|\to\mathbb{E}|C^\star(X)|$ while (5) holds for every $n$.*

Four consequences guide the method.

*The optimal score.* The size-optimal score is the conditional density, $s(x,y)=-f(y\,|\, x)$, combined with stratum-specific thresholds; a single threshold is optimal only for marginal coverage.

*The price of stratum-wise validity.* Every set that satisfies the stratum constraints has marginal coverage at least $1-\bar\alpha$, $\bar\alpha=\sum_kp_k\alpha_k$. Hence $\mathbb{E}|C^\star(X)|$ is at least the expected size of the size-optimal marginal set at level $1-\bar\alpha$, a single-threshold density level set; under the assumptions of Proposition 2 the two coincide if and only if $t_1=\dots=t_K$, i.e. if and only if that marginal set already has coverage $1-\alpha_k$ in every stratum. With a common level $\alpha_k=\alpha$ this difference is the price, in size, of stratum-wise validity.

*Coverage is reallocated across inputs.* In a location–scale model $f(y\,|\, x)=g\{(y-\mu(x))/\sigma(x)\}/\sigma(x)$ the optimal set is, in standardised units $u=(y-\mu(x))/\sigma(x)$, $\{u: g(u)\ge t_k\sigma(x)\}$ within stratum $k$. Its standardised width therefore *shrinks* as $\sigma(x)$ grows, and it is empty whenever $t_k\sigma(x)>\max g$: the oracle spends coverage on inputs where it is cheap and withholds it where it is expensive. The stratified normalised residual (S-NCP), whose standardised width does not depend on $x$, is optimal only when $\sigma$ is constant. The size advantage of the density score over S-NCP thus comes partly from the factor $1/\sigma(x)$ and is paid for by lower coverage, and occasionally empty sets, for the noisiest inputs. Stratum-wise coverage is unaffected, but covariate-conditional coverage across levels of $\sigma(x)$ is not equalised (Section 6 reports both). Users who need comparable coverage across noise levels should prefer S-NCP.

*Shape.* $C^\star(x)$ need not be an interval even when $f(\cdot\,|\, x)$ is unimodal: thresholds differ across strata (typically $t_k$ is smaller in a rare tail stratum), so $C^\star(x)$ can contain a piece of a tail stratum while excluding part of an adjacent stratum.

*Scope.* The optimality refers to size averaged over the covariate law $P_X$ of the calibration population; under a shifted $Q$, $C^\star$ remains valid in the sense of Corollary 1(i) but is no longer size-optimal. For targets with atoms (ties, zero inflation) Theorem 1 is unaffected, whereas Lebesgue size is degenerate at an atom and the size-optimal set contains every atom; on such data a smooth density score is valid but not size-optimal.

## 4.3 The SDLC procedure

Figure 1 summarises the workflow: a density model and the target strata are learned on the proper training set, and a separate conformal threshold is calibrated within each stratum on the calibration set.

![**Fig. 1.** Workflow of stratified density-level conformal prediction (SDLC). Upper row: the location–scale density model $\hat f(y\,|\, x)$ and the target strata $B_1,\dots,B_K$ are fitted on the proper training set. Lower row: calibration scores are grouped by the stratum of the observed target and a conformal quantile $\hat q_k$ is computed in each stratum. The prediction set is the union over strata of the density level sets at the stratum's threshold, which guarantees coverage within every stratum in finite samples (Theorem 1).](/home/user/p_c1_2/Target_Journal_Submission/figures/F0_method_schematic.png){width=100%}


SDLC instantiates (3)–(4) with the plug-in score $s(x,y)=-\hat f(y\,|\, x)$, where $\hat f$ is estimated on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$. By Theorem 1 the guarantee (5) holds for any $\hat f$; Propositions 2 and 3 motivate the score. We use the semiparametric location–scale estimator
$$\hat f(y\,|\, x)=\frac{1}{\hat\sigma(x)}\,\hat g\!\left(\frac{y-\hat\mu(x)}{\hat\sigma(x)}\right). \qquad (8)$$
Here $\hat\mu$ is a gradient-boosted regression of $Y$ on $X$, and $\hat\sigma(x)=\max\{\tilde\sigma(x),\varsigma\}$, where $\tilde\sigma$ is a gradient-boosted regression of the absolute *out-of-fold* residuals $|Y-\hat\mu_{-j}(X)|$ (three folds), the spread estimate of locally weighted conformal prediction [@lei2018distribution]. The floor $\varsigma=\max\{10^{-3}\operatorname{sd}(Y_{\mathrm{tr}}),\,0.05\,\mathrm{median}|Y-\hat\mu_{-j}(X)|\}$ is shared by every method that uses $\hat\sigma$. The noise density is a defensive mixture
$$\hat g=(1-\epsilon)\,\hat g_{\mathrm{KDE}}+\epsilon\,t_2(\cdot/\hat s)/\hat s,\qquad \epsilon=0.01, \qquad (9)$$
of a Gaussian kernel density estimate (Silverman bandwidth) and a Student-$t_2$ density with robust scale $\hat s=\mathrm{IQR}/1.349$. The mixture is fitted to *cross-fitted* standardised residuals: the out-of-fold residuals of fold $j$ are divided by a scale model trained on the other two folds, so that neither $\hat\mu$ nor $\hat\sigma$ has seen the residual it standardises. (Standardising by an in-sample scale fit shrinks the residuals, in our data by a median factor of 1.14 in standard deviation. Conversely, cross-fitted residuals come from models trained on two thirds of the data and are somewhat wider than the residuals of the final models, so $\hat g$ is mildly too wide for test-time residuals. Neither misfit affects validity, which calibration guarantees; both affect only efficiency, and we did not tune this choice.) The defensive component gives $\hat g$ polynomial tails, so that targets far outside the training residuals still receive a smooth, strictly decreasing density rather than an arbitrary floor. The noise density is nonparametric, so (8) captures skewness and heavy tails of the error distribution, and heteroscedasticity enters through $\hat\sigma$. If $Y=\mu(X)+\sigma(X)\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon$ independent of $X$, and the defensive weight $\epsilon$ and the floor $\varsigma$ shrink to zero as $N$ grows, (8) can be uniformly consistent and Proposition 3 applies. Our implementation keeps $\epsilon=0.01$ and the floor fixed and truncates to the domain $D$, so Proposition 3 describes the limit of a sequence of such estimators, not the fixed-constant implementation itself. Otherwise SDLC targets the optimal sets of the best location–scale approximation, which affects size but never validity.

Three alternatives are evaluated as ablations. SDLC-N replaces $\hat g$ by the standard normal density, with a defensive $t_2$ component of unit scale, so that its score differs from the normalised residual mainly through the factor $1/\hat\sigma(x)$. Because $\hat\sigma$ estimates the mean absolute residual rather than the standard deviation, the standardised residuals have a standard deviation above one, so SDLC-N is not exactly the Gaussian-noise version of SDLC and isolates the role of $1/\hat\sigma(x)$ only approximately. An exploratory *two-piece* variant replaces $\hat\sigma$ by side-specific scales $\hat\sigma_-(x)$ below $\hat\mu(x)$ and $\hat\sigma_+(x)$ above it. A fully nonparametric histogram density is obtained from a multiclass gradient-boosted classifier over 40 equal-mass target bins (a "regression-as-classification" density, cf. @sesia2021conformal and @guha2024conformal).

## 4.4 Implementation

*Strata.* The primary stratification uses the training-target quantiles $0.10, 0.25, 0.75, 0.90$ ($K=5$: lower tail, lower shoulder, bulk, upper shoulder, upper tail). With tied targets coinciding cut points are merged and empty strata removed, so the realised number of strata and their masses can differ from the nominal values; realised values are reported per dataset, and the sensitivity analyses repeat the main comparisons on the datasets whose realised tail strata are close to nominal.

*Exact scores and the domain.* Calibration scores are computed exactly at the observed target, $S_i=-\hat f(Y_i\,|\, X_i)$. Sets are restricted to a bounded domain $D=[\min y-0.05r,\max y+0.05r]$ of the training targets ($r$ the training range), intersected with $[0,\infty)$ when all training targets are non-negative. A point with $Y\notin D$ receives the score $+\infty$, stays in its stratum, and counts as not covered. Membership of a test target is decided by the same exact rule, $Y_{n+1}\in\hat C(X_{n+1})$ iff $s(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})\le\hat q_{k(Y_{n+1})}$, so the lower bound of Theorem 1 applies to the reported coverage whenever $\hat q_k$ is finite; a test target outside $D$ counts as a miss even when $\hat q_k=+\infty$, so the reported tail coverage can fall slightly below the bound on datasets with test targets outside $D$. Two details follow from the domain. First, $\hat q_k=+\infty$ also when the $r_k$-th calibration score of stratum $k$ is $+\infty$, and the reported set is then $B_k\cap D$ rather than $B_k$. Second, the upper bound of Theorem 1 requires $P(Y\notin D)=0$. We report the fraction of test targets outside $D$ and how often $\hat q_k=+\infty$ occurs.

*Set geometry.* Only the size, the number of pieces, the hull and the tail alerts of a set are computed numerically. For a test point, $\hat C(x)\cap B_k=\{\hat\mu(x)+\hat\sigma(x)u:\hat g(u)\ge-\hat q_k\hat\sigma(x)\}\cap B_k$. We tabulate $\hat g$ on a non-uniform standardised grid (dense over the bulk of the residuals, logarithmically spaced beyond, analytic $t$ tails outside), interpolate linearly within each cell, and split every cell exactly at the stratum boundaries and the bounds of $D$. The reported geometry is therefore that of the stratum-exact set for the piecewise-linear interpolant of $\hat g$. A grid-convergence check on six large or heavy-tailed datasets (Supplementary Section S3, Table S4) shows that a four times finer grid changes mean set sizes by at most 0.2% and individual set sizes by at most 1.5%. The same construction is used for every location–scale baseline (DLS, DCP, HPD-split, S-HPD, SDLC-N). Interval methods report half-open stratum pieces, so that a candidate on a boundary belongs to exactly one stratum, as in (4). For density-based sets the reported stratum pieces are closed, so with tied targets a cut point can appear in two adjacent pieces; this changes neither the size (a single point) nor the reported coverage, which is decided by the exact rule above.

**Algorithm 1.** Stratified density-level conformal prediction (SDLC).

> *Input:* proper training set $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$; calibration set $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$; stratum levels $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_K$; stratum probabilities (e.g. $0.10,0.25,0.75,0.90$).
> *Output:* prediction set $\hat C(x)$ for any test input $x$.
>
> 1. Fit the mean model $\hat\mu$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and compute three-fold out-of-fold residuals $Y-\hat\mu_{-j}(X)$.
> 2. Fit the scale model $\tilde\sigma$ on all absolute out-of-fold residuals and three cross-fitted scale models; set $\hat\sigma=\max\{\tilde\sigma,\varsigma\}$.
> 3. Standardise the out-of-fold residuals with the cross-fitted scales and fit the noise density $\hat g$ by Eq. (9); form $\hat f(y\,|\, x)$ by Eq. (8).
> 4. Compute the cut points from the training targets and define the strata $B_1,\dots,B_K$ and the domain $D$.
> 5. **for** each $(X_i,Y_i)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$ **do** $S_i\leftarrow-\hat f(Y_i\,|\, X_i)$ if $Y_i\in D$, else $S_i\leftarrow+\infty$; assign $i$ to stratum $k(Y_i)$.
> 6. **for** $k=1,\dots,K$ **do** $\hat q_k\leftarrow$ the $\lceil (n_k+1)(1-\alpha_k)\rceil$-th smallest score in stratum $k$ (Eq. 3; $+\infty$ if this rank exceeds $n_k$).
> 7. **return** $\hat C(x)=\bigcup_{k}\{y\in B_k\cap D:\hat f(y\,|\, x)\ge-\hat q_k\}$, computed stratum-exactly on the standardised grid.

## 4.5 Computational complexity

Fitting (steps 1–3) requires eight gradient-boosted regressions: one mean model, three out-of-fold mean models, one full-data scale model and three cross-fitted scale models (our benchmark code refits four of them in a separate cross-fitting stage and therefore fits twelve). The kernel density estimate costs $O(N+G_g\log G_g)$ by linear binning and FFT convolution on $G_g$ grid points, where $N$ is the number of residuals. Calibration (steps 5–6) costs $O(n\log n)$ for $n$ calibration points, dominated by sorting within strata. Prediction (step 7) inverts the density level on each of the $R$ monotone runs of the piecewise-linear $\hat g$ ($R\le G$, typically a handful) within each of the $K$ strata, at a cost of $O(KR\log G)$ per test point; the total prediction cost is therefore linear in the number of test points, and one fit serves every miscoverage level $\alpha$. Measured wall-clock times of all methods are reported in Section 6.9 and Supplementary Tables S13 and S15.

## 4.6 Two practical variants

*Hull variant (SDLC-hull).* When an interval is required, the convex hull of $\hat C(x)$ is reported. It contains $\hat C(x)$ and therefore inherits (5); the upper bound of Theorem 1 and the optimality of Proposition 2 do not carry over, and when some $\hat q_k=+\infty$ the hull spans most of $D$.

*Floor variant (SDLC-floor).* A user may want the efficiency of a single marginal threshold while protecting rare strata at a lower level $1-\beta$. The set $\hat C_{\mathrm{floor}}(x)=\{y: s(x,y)\le\max(\hat q^{\mathrm{marg}}_\alpha,\hat q_{k(y)}(\beta))\}$, with $\hat q^{\mathrm{marg}}_\alpha$ the ordinary split-conformal quantile at level $\alpha$ and $\hat q_k(\beta)$ the stratified quantile (3) at level $\beta$, contains both the marginal set and the stratified set at level $\beta$. It therefore has marginal coverage $\ge 1-\alpha$ and stratum-wise coverage $\ge 1-\beta$ simultaneously, in finite samples and for any $\beta\in(0,1)$; we use $\beta=2\alpha$. This is a simple and conservative rule, not a contribution of the paper. Level-optimising schemes that trade coverage between bins [@wang2023equal] or choose group-specific levels to minimise length [@kiyani2024length] are principled alternatives.

## 4.7 Relevance-weighted coverage without strata: RW-DLC

Stratified calibration protects each stratum, but its protection ends at the resolution of the strata: outcomes in the top 1% lie inside the upper-tail stratum and are covered only on average with the rest of that stratum, and refining the strata quickly leaves too few calibration points per stratum. The imbalanced-regression literature describes the importance of outcomes not by strata but by a *relevance function* $\phi:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)$, for example the inverse of the target density [@torgo2007utility; @steininger2021denseweight]. We therefore also consider *relevance-weighted coverage*
$$\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(C)=\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\phi(Y)\,\mathbf{1}\{Y\in C(X)\}\big]}{\mathbb{E}[\phi(Y)]}, \qquad (10)$$
which, for $\phi=1/p_Y$, is coverage averaged uniformly over the range of the target, the continuous analogue of the macro-coverage that @bhattacharyya2026macro study for long-tailed classification. A stratum-wise guarantee for every stratum implies (10) for every $\phi$ that is constant on strata; (10) instead gives one averaged guarantee for a continuous relevance, without choosing strata.

Let $\phi$ be fixed given $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$, let $S_i=s(X_i,Y_i)$ be the calibration scores with weights $\phi_i=\phi(Y_i)$ and $W=\sum_i\phi_i$, and define for a candidate $y$
$$q(y)=\inf\Big\{s:\ \sum_{i=1}^n\phi_i\,\mathbf{1}\{S_i\le s\}\ \ge\ (1-\alpha)\big(W+\phi(y)\big)\Big\},\qquad \hat C_\phi(x)=\{y:\ s(x,y)\le q(y)\}, \qquad (11)$$
with $q(y)=+\infty$ when no such $s$ exists. The candidate's own weight sits at $+\infty$, as in weighted conformal prediction under covariate shift [@tibshirani2019conformal] and label shift [@podkopaev2021distribution].

**Theorem 2 (relevance-weighted validity).** *If $(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,(X_n,Y_n),(X,Y)$ are i.i.d. given $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and $0<\mathbb{E}\phi(Y)<\infty$, then $\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(\hat C_\phi)\big]\ge 1-\alpha$, the expectation being over $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$. Equivalently, $\hat C_\phi$ has coverage at least $1-\alpha$ for a test point drawn from the target-shifted law $Q$ with $dQ/dP=\phi(y)/\mathbb{E}\phi(Y)$.*

The proof (Appendix A) applies the weighted-exchangeability argument of @tibshirani2019conformal with the likelihood ratio $\phi(y)/\mathbb{E}\phi(Y)$, which depends only on the target. Two consequences matter in practice. First, (11) gives $q(y)=+\infty$ exactly when $\phi(y)>\alpha W/(1-\alpha)$: a candidate whose relevance exceeds the fraction $\alpha/(1-\alpha)$ of the total calibration relevance is always included. Rare targets are protected without strata, but only to the extent that the calibration set contains comparable relevance mass, which the Kish effective sample size $W^2/\sum_i\phi_i^2$ summarises. Second, Theorem 2 is also an exact guarantee under a target shift whose density ratio is proportional to $\phi$.

**Proposition 4 (size-optimal relevance-weighted sets).** *Under the density assumption of Proposition 2, among all set-valued functions with $\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(C)\ge1-\alpha$, the expected Lebesgue size $\mathbb{E}|C(X)|$ is minimised by $C^\star_\phi(x)=\{y:\ \phi(y)f(y\,|\, x)\ge t\}$, with $t$ chosen so that the constraint holds with equality (assuming the distribution of $\phi(Y)f(Y\,|\, X)$ has no atom at $t$).*

The proof is the Neyman–Pearson argument of Proposition 2 with the measure $\phi(y)f(y\,|\, x)\,dy\,dP_X$ in place of the stratum measure; its discrete counterpart for class-weighted coverage is given by @bhattacharyya2026macro. The proposition yields the score of *relevance-weighted density-level conformal prediction* (RW-DLC), $s(x,y)=-\phi(y)\hat f(y\,|\, x)$, calibrated by (11) and using the same density estimate (8) as SDLC. Relevance thus enters twice: it tilts the score towards relevant outcomes, which shapes the sets, and it weights the calibration, which delivers the guarantee. In the experiments $\phi$ is fitted on the training targets only, $\phi_U(y)=1/\max\{\hat p_Y(y),\tau\}$ ("uniform over the target range") or $\phi_H=\phi_U^{1/2}$, with $\hat p_Y$ a Gaussian kernel density estimate and $\tau=0.01\max_i\hat p_Y(Y_i)$ (Section 5).


# 5. Experimental design

The study answers six research questions (RQs), each tied to one experiment. The datasets, seeds, splits, miscoverage levels, strata, base learners, method list and analyses of the main benchmark were fixed in a version-controlled configuration before the benchmark was run; Section 5.7 states what was and what was not fixed in advance.

* **RQ1 (tail coverage).** Do marginal and covariate-adaptive conformal methods under-cover rare target strata on real data, and does stratified calibration restore stratum-wise coverage? *Experiment 1: main benchmark.*
* **RQ2 (price).** How much larger are the sets when stratum-wise validity is required, compared with marginal methods at the same and at a stricter miscoverage level? *Experiment 2: paired comparison of each score under marginal and stratified calibration.*
* **RQ3 (score).** Does the density-level score of Proposition 2 give smaller stratified sets than residual, normalised-residual, quantile and HPD scores, and which component of the score is responsible? *Experiment 3: score ablation and decomposition on real data, a synthetic study with known densities, and a confirmatory replication on held-out datasets.*
* **RQ4 (shift).** How robust is stratum-wise coverage when the target distribution shifts? *Experiment 4: a positive-control shift that re-weights whole strata (Corollary 1(i) applies), continuous tilts that also re-weight within strata (it does not), and a genuine label shift in the synthetic study.*
* **RQ5 (decisions).** Are prediction sets useful for flagging extreme outcomes, compared with dedicated conformal tail classifiers and conformal selection? *Experiment 5: tail-alert decision study with expected-cost analysis.*
* **RQ6 (robustness).** How sensitive are the conclusions to the miscoverage level, the strata, the calibration-set size, the density estimator, the base learner and imbalance-aware training? *Experiment 6: sensitivity analyses.* Computational cost and an error analysis complete the evaluation.

## 5.1 Datasets

The main benchmark uses the OpenML-CTR23 suite, a curated benchmark of 35 tabular regression datasets with 500–100,000 observations and documented provenance [@fischer2023openml]. Using a complete, externally curated suite avoids selecting datasets that favour the proposed method. We excluded, by a rule fixed in advance, the four datasets whose target takes fewer than 20 distinct values (red and white wine quality, solar flares, student performance), because for ordinal targets with a handful of values Lebesgue set size does not measure informativeness. The rule does not remove targets with many distinct values but heavy ties or atoms: weekly working hours (health insurance) and the burned area of forest fires, which is zero for 47.8% of the fires, are retained and analysed separately because their realised strata depart from the nominal ones. The remaining 31 datasets (Supplementary Table S1) range from 517 to 72,000 observations and from 5 to 971 features after one-hot encoding; 15 have a target skewness above 1 in absolute value. Categorical features were one-hot encoded and missing values were passed to the gradient-boosting models, which handle them natively.

*Confirmatory datasets.* For the replication (Experiment 3), we assembled before any confirmatory computation the regression datasets of the tabular benchmark of @grinsztajn2022tree (OpenML suites 335 and 336). We removed every dataset that shares source data with a CTR23 dataset (for example house sales, Miami housing, superconductivity and diamonds) and duplicate versions, and applied the same exclusion rule. This left 17 held-out datasets with up to 50,000 observations (larger datasets were subsampled once, with a fixed seed); they are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

## 5.2 Splitting, leakage control and repetitions

Each of the 10 predefined seeds (0–9) defines a random partition into proper training (50%), calibration (25%) and test (25%) sets; the replication uses seeds 0–4. Every model, every stratum boundary and every density estimate uses the proper training set only; early stopping uses a 10% internal validation split of the proper training set, and calibration and test data are never used for fitting. Random splitting makes the rows of a dataset exchangeable, which is all that Theorem 1 requires, but several datasets contain repeated units: exact duplicate rows (for example 9.1% of sarcos, whose test set was appended to its training set), many transcodings of the same video (video transcoding), repeat sales (King County) and team-season panels (Moneyball). The guarantees therefore refer to a new row drawn from the same pool, not to a new video, a new house or a future season; Supplementary Section S2 reports the leakage audit (Table S3). Three small datasets (airfoil, concrete, QSAR fish toxicity) were used during development to choose the density estimator, and seven further datasets were used to diagnose the implementation (Section 5.7); the main comparisons are repeated without these ten datasets.

## 5.3 Base learners

All methods share the same LightGBM models [@ke2017lightgbm] with a single fixed configuration (learning rate 0.05, 31 leaves, minimum 20 samples per leaf, row and column subsampling 0.8, $\ell_2$ penalty 1, up to 2000 rounds with early stopping after 50 rounds): a mean model $\hat\mu$, quantile models at $\alpha/2$ and $1-\alpha/2$ (for CQR), the scale models of Algorithm 1, and a multiclass model over 40 equal-mass target bins (histogram density). No method receives method-specific tuning, so differences between methods reflect the conformal procedures and not the base learners. Two further learner settings are used in Experiment 6: a two-layer perceptron with the same pipeline (median imputation with missingness indicators, cross-fitted scales and the same floor), and LightGBM trained with DenseWeight sample weights, which up-weight rare targets [@steininger2021denseweight].

## 5.4 Compared methods

*Marginal or covariate-adaptive calibration (baselines).* Split conformal prediction with absolute residuals (SCP) [@papadopoulos2002inductive; @lei2018distribution]; normalised (locally weighted) conformal prediction (NCP) [@lei2018distribution]; conformalised quantile regression (CQR) [@romano2019conformalized]; Mondrian conformal regression with five equal-mass bins of the point prediction (Mondrian-ŷ), in the spirit of @bostrom2020mondrian and @boruah2026distribution; distributional conformal prediction (DCP) [@chernozhukov2021distributional]; HPD-split [@izbicki2022cdsplit]; and density level sets with a single threshold on the conditional density (DLS) [cf. @lei2013distribution; @izbicki2020flexible]. DCP, HPD-split and DLS use the same location–scale density as SDLC, so the comparison with SDLC isolates the calibration rule.

*Stratified calibration.* BCCP [@randahl2026bin], i.e. the absolute-residual score with stratum-wise thresholds, in its union form and its hull ("contiguized") form (BCCP-hull); the normalised-residual score with stratum-wise thresholds (S-NCP), in union and hull form; stratified CQR (S-CQR), the CQR score with stratum-wise thresholds; the HPD score with stratum-wise thresholds (S-HPD); and the proposed SDLC with its hull and floor ($\beta=2\alpha$) variants. S-CQR is related to the binned calibration of @wang2023equal but omits its level optimisation and uses strata from training quantiles, so it should not be read as an implementation of BFQR. S-NCP is the information-matched comparator of SDLC: both use the same $\hat\mu$ and $\hat\sigma$, and SDLC adds the noise density.

*Ablations of the score.* SDLC-N replaces the kernel estimate $\hat g$ by a Gaussian density, so that the chain BCCP → S-NCP → SDLC-N → SDLC adds, in turn, the scale $\hat\sigma(x)$, the density weighting $1/\hat\sigma(x)$, and the estimated noise shape. Two alternative densities are reported as secondary results: the multiclass histogram density and an exploratory two-piece location–scale density.

*Dedicated tail-alert rules (Experiment 5).* A set raises an *upper-tail alert* when it intersects the upper stratum (outcomes above the training 90% quantile); for every method with stratum-wise coverage $1-\alpha$ the alert recall is at least $1-\alpha$. We compare such alerts with three rules built from the same models: a class-conditional conformal tail classifier (TC-LS), which thresholds the probability $\hat P(Y\in B_K\,|\, x)$ of the location–scale model so that the recall is at least $1-\alpha$; the same classifier with the probability from the histogram model (TC-H); and conformal selection with Benjamini–Hochberg control of the false discovery rate at $q\in\{0.1,0.2\}$ [@jin2023selection]. The naive rule "alert if $\hat\mu(x)$ is in the tail" is included as a reference without guarantee.

*Implementation.* All methods are fitted once per dataset and seed. Model outputs are cached, and every method is evaluated from the same cached outputs with the exact rules of Section 4.4.

## 5.5 Evaluation metrics

For each test set we report:

* marginal coverage, and stratum-wise coverage (1) in each of the $K$ calibration strata;
* coverage in strata *not* used for calibration: the training-target deciles, the top and bottom 5% and 1%, and the deciles of $\hat\sigma(x)$, the last as a diagnostic of covariate-conditional coverage together with worst-slab coverage (WSC) [@cauchois2021knowing];
* mean set size divided by the training-target standard deviation (normalised size), the share of empty sets and the mean number of disjoint pieces per set;
* tail-alert recall, alert rate and precision, and the expected cost per unit when a missed extreme outcome costs $r$ times a false alert;
* coverage under shift, computed by importance weighting of the test set: tilts $w(y)=\exp(\gamma z_y)$ towards the upper tail ($\gamma\in\{1,2\}$), the lower tail and both tails, where $z_y$ is the normal score of $y$ under the training distribution, and a *positive control* that multiplies the upper stratum by 5 and the lower stratum by 0.5 without changing the within-stratum laws, so that Corollary 1(i) applies exactly. We report the Kish effective sample size of the weights and repeat the shift analysis on the datasets where it is at least 50.

Stratum coverages are averaged over seeds before taking the minimum across strata ("worst-stratum coverage"). This reduces, but does not remove, the downward bias of a minimum over noisy estimates [cf. @boruah2026null]. We therefore also report a *null reference*: the worst-stratum coverage expected under exact conformal validity, given the realised calibration and test stratum sizes (Monte Carlo over the Beta–binomial law of split-conformal coverage). Computing time is recorded for every fit and for calibration plus prediction.

## 5.6 Statistical analysis

Following @demsar2006statistical, the unit of analysis is the dataset: metrics are averaged over seeds within each dataset and methods are compared across datasets. Set sizes are compared on the log scale and summarised by the geometric-mean ratio with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval (10,000 resamples of datasets), together with the Hodges–Lehmann estimate and its Wilcoxon-inversion confidence interval, the numbers of wins and losses, and the sign test. Paired comparisons use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Holm's step-down correction within each family [@holm1979simple], and the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation as effect size. One-sided alternatives are used only for the directional hypothesis that stratified calibration improves worst-stratum and tail coverage over marginal methods; all comparisons among stratified methods are two-sided. Equivalence of worst-stratum coverage between stratified methods is assessed by two one-sided Wilcoxon tests (TOST) with a margin of 0.01. Friedman mean ranks are reported descriptively only, because mean-rank post-hoc comparisons depend on which other methods are included [@benavoli2016should].

## 5.7 Protocol, deviations and confirmatory replication

*What was fixed in advance.* The configuration of the main benchmark (datasets, exclusion rule, seeds, split proportions, miscoverage levels $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2\}$, strata, base learners, the original method list and the sensitivity analyses), the primary method (SDLC with the location–scale density) and directional expectations for RQ1–RQ6 were committed to version control before the benchmark was run. These expectations were not registered with a third party, and we do not call the study pre-specified.

*What changed afterwards.* (1) An audit found that string-typed features had been parsed as missing values (month and day in the forest-fire data); the loader was corrected and every dataset with string-typed features was re-processed and recomputed. (2) After the first run we learned of BCCP and BFQR; BCCP's hull form, the decile-stratified variants and tail-alert metrics were added. (3) The first implementation evaluated all density-based sets on a fixed grid of 200 equal-mass cells of the *marginal* target distribution, which inflates sets whenever the conditional density is narrower than a cell, for baselines and proposed method alike. Under that implementation the size comparison with BCCP was not significant (Supplementary Section S5). (4) A review of the second run identified further weaknesses that we corrected for every method: the noise density was fitted to residuals standardised by an in-sample scale model (now cross-fitted), the density had an ad hoc floor (now a defensive $t$ component), interval methods used closed stratum pieces (now half-open), the domain included impossible negative values (now truncated), and the set geometry was computed on a per-$x$ grid (now stratum-exact). All reported results use the final implementation; earlier results are archived with the code.

*Consequences.* Because the implementation changed after results had been seen, every size comparison on CTR23 (RQ3) is treated as exploratory. The coverage results (RQ1, RQ2, RQ4) are unaffected in direction by any of these changes. To test the size comparisons, we fixed a confirmatory analysis plan (hypotheses, tests, decision rules and analysis script; Supplementary Section S6) and committed it to version control (commit `ccba99b`) *before* evaluating any conformal method on the 17 held-out datasets. The time stamp is that of the authors' repository, not of a third-party registry, so we call the plan pre-specified rather than pre-specified; base-model test metrics (accuracy and test log-likelihood of the fitted models) had already been computed on these datasets when the plan was fixed. Section 6.5 reports the outcome whatever its direction.

*Relevance-weighted study.* RW-DLC (Section 4.7) was added after review of an earlier version. Its analysis plan (relevance functions, constants, comparators, metrics, four hypotheses RW-1 to RW-4, tests and decision rules; Supplementary Section S7) was committed to version control (commit `a9f31d2`) before RW-DLC was evaluated on any dataset, and the study was then run on both the CTR23 datasets and the held-out datasets. RW-DLC, its ablations RW-DLS (score $-\hat f$ with relevance-weighted calibration) and RW-NCP (normalised residual with relevance-weighted calibration), and the comparators SCP, NCP, CQR, DLS-M, S-NCP and SDLC were all evaluated on a common grid of 4,000 equally spaced target values over $D$, with coverage decided by the exact rule. Additional metrics are the relevance-weighted coverage (10), estimated on test points with $\phi$ evaluated at the test targets, the mean and minimum coverage over the ten deciles of the training targets, and coverage of the top and bottom 1% of outcomes.

## 6.1 Marginal and covariate-adaptive methods under-cover the tails (RQ1)

All methods attained their nominal marginal coverage. Coverage of the target strata, however, differed sharply. The seven marginal or covariate-adaptive methods covered 69.0%–82.0% of the upper-tail targets and 44.5%–65.5% of the top 1%, and their worst-stratum coverage was 63.0%–78.3%. On 26–31 of the 31 datasets, it was below the range expected under exact validity. Fig. 2 shows the pattern: marginal methods over-cover the central deciles and pay for it in the outer ones, as Section 3 predicts. Covariate adaptivity reduces the shortfall (NCP, DCP and HPD-split are better than SCP and CQR) but does not remove it. Every stratified method, in contrast, covered 90.6%–90.9% of the upper tail, with worst-stratum coverage 89.0%–89.3%, and was below the null range on at most 2 datasets, as expected under exact validity. SDLC's worst-stratum coverage exceeded that of each marginal method on at least 29 of 31 datasets (one-sided Holm-adjusted $p\le$ < 0.001; Hodges–Lehmann differences 0.081–0.250). The result is unchanged without the development datasets ($p\le$ < 0.001) and for upper-tail coverage alone ($p\le$ < 0.001). Protection extends partly beyond the calibrated strata: SDLC covered 86.8% of the top 5% and 73.6% of the top 1%, against at most 77.0% and 65.5% for the marginal methods. The top 1% is not calibrated, however, and its coverage remains below the nominal level for every method.

## 6.2 The price of stratum-wise validity (RQ2)

Stratum-wise validity enlarges the sets (Table 4). Holding the score fixed, stratified calibration increased the geometric-mean size by factors of 1.17 (normalised residual) to 1.27 (absolute residual). For SDLC relative to the single-threshold density level set (DLS) the factor was 1.24 (1.12–1.45), with a median of 1.11, for a gain in worst-stratum coverage of 0.231 (between 0.126 and 0.260 across scores). The price was concentrated: it exceeded 1.5 on only 3 datasets for SDLC (Section 6.10).

A fairer view of the price compares stratified sets with marginal sets that buy tail coverage by lowering $\alpha$ (Table 5). At $\alpha=0.05$ every marginal method still had a lower worst-stratum coverage (0.772–0.878) than SDLC at $\alpha=0.1$ (0.891), and SDLC's sets were smaller than theirs on 23–28 of the 31 datasets. Raising the overall coverage level is therefore an inefficient way to protect the tails: calibrating within strata gives more tail coverage for less size.

## 6.3 The density score gives the smallest stratum-valid sets (RQ3)

Among the stratified methods, which all had equivalent worst-stratum coverage (TOST with margin 0.01: $p\le$ < 0.001 for every comparison with SDLC), SDLC produced the smallest sets (Table 6, Fig. 3). Its sets were smaller than those of BCCP on 24 of 31 datasets (geometric-mean ratio 0.902, 95% CI 0.841–0.956; Holm-adjusted $p$ < 0.001), than those of stratified CQR on 27 (ratio 0.795) and than those of S-HPD on 28 (ratio 0.947). Against the information-matched S-NCP, which uses the same $\hat\mu$ and $\hat\sigma$, SDLC was smaller on 27 datasets (Holm $p$ < 0.001; Hodges–Lehmann ratio 0.962), but the gain was small and its geometric-mean ratio, 0.975 (0.941–1.020), was pulled towards one by a few datasets with large losses (Section 6.10). The two summaries answer different questions: the Hodges–Lehmann ratio and the signed-rank test describe the typical dataset, the geometric mean the average log ratio. We declare the former as the primary size estimand and report the latter as a secondary summary. Post hoc, an equivalence test with a ±5% margin on the log scale did not establish equivalence of SDLC with S-NCP (TOST $p$ = 0.108) or with S-HPD ($p$ = 0.284). Judged dataset by dataset, SDLC's sets were reliably smaller than S-NCP's (seed-level 95% interval of the log ratio below zero) on 18 of 31 datasets and reliably larger on 0; against BCCP the counts were 17 and 0. When an interval is required, SDLC-hull was smaller than BCCP-hull (ratio 0.903, 25/6), whereas against S-NCP-hull the median favoured SDLC-hull (23/8, Hodges–Lehmann ratio 0.979) and the geometric mean did not (1.014). Because the implementation of the density-based methods was changed after earlier results had been seen (Section 5.7), we treat these comparisons as exploratory; Section 6.5 tests them on held-out data.

The advantage was robust to the design choices (Supplementary Table S5). SDLC remained smaller than all four stratified comparators without the ten development and diagnostic datasets, on the 26 datasets whose realised tail strata are close to nominal, at $\alpha=0.05$ and $\alpha=0.2$, and with three or ten strata (Holm $p\le$ 0.002 in each of these analyses). Two subsets were less clear. On the 9 datasets with fewer than 1,600 observations no stratified score was consistently better (SDLC vs S-NCP: 5/4). On the 15 strongly skewed datasets ($|$skewness$|>1$), SDLC was smaller than BCCP and S-HPD, while its advantage over S-NCP (12/3, Holm $p$ = 0.127) and S-CQR ($p$ = 0.127) was not significant.

*Where the gain comes from.* The ablation chain in Table 6 and Fig. 4 attributes the gain. Adding the scale $\hat\sigma(x)$ to the residual score (BCCP → S-NCP) reduced size on average (ratio 0.926) but not consistently (18/13 datasets, $p$ = 0.111). ## 6.5 Confirmatory replication on held-out datasets

On the 17 held-out datasets, analysed exactly as pre-specified (Supplementary Section S6), all four hypotheses were supported (Table 8).

* *C-H1 (tail coverage).* SDLC's worst-stratum coverage exceeded that of each of the seven marginal and covariate-adaptive methods (Holm $p$ < 0.001).
* *C-H2 (validity).* Its worst-stratum coverage fell below the 5% quantile of the exact-validity null reference on 1 of 17 datasets (critical value 3).
* *C-H3 (set size).* SDLC's sets were smaller than those of BCCP (geometric-mean ratio 0.862, 14/3 datasets, Holm $p$ = 0.001), S-CQR (0.841, 15/2, $p$ < 0.001), S-NCP (0.977, 15/2, $p$ = 0.008) and S-HPD (0.984, 15/2, $p$ = 0.008). By the pre-specified rule, which combines the signed-rank test with the direction of the geometric-mean ratio, every comparison favours SDLC. The gains over S-NCP and S-HPD are again small and consistent: the bootstrap intervals of their geometric-mean ratios (0.940–1.028 and 0.963–1.014) include one, while the signed-rank and sign tests show that the reduction occurs on most datasets.
* *C-H4 (equivalence).* The worst-stratum coverage of SDLC was equivalent to that of S-NCP and BCCP within 0.02 (TOST $p$ < 0.001 and < 0.001).

C-H1, C-H2 and C-H4 follow from Theorem 1 and the definition of marginal methods, so they check the implementation rather than test a new claim; C-H3 is the only hypothesis that could have failed for empirical reasons.

The secondary results repeated the trade-off seen on CTR23. SDLC covered 0.747 of the top 1%, against 0.781 for S-NCP, and its worst coverage over deciles of $\hat\sigma(x)$ was 0.817, against 0.831. The price of stratification relative to DLS was 1.22, close to that on CTR23. The pre-specified secondary analyses (Supplementary Table S17) were less favourable for the interval forms: SDLC-hull was smaller than BCCP-hull (ratio 0.894, Holm $p$ = 0.013) but not than S-NCP-hull (ratio 1.045, 10/7 datasets, $p$ = 0.712). Post hoc, SDLC was equivalent to S-HPD within ±5% (TOST $p$ = 0.004), equivalence with S-NCP narrowly failed ($p$ = 0.054), and SDLC was reliably smaller than S-NCP on 8 of 17 datasets and reliably larger on 1. Only 7 of the held-out targets have $|$skewness$|>1$; on them the advantage over S-NCP and S-HPD disappeared (ratios 1.017 and 1.004), as on the skewed CTR23 datasets, while the advantage over BCCP and S-CQR remained (Supplementary Table S18). Among scores that use $\hat\sigma$, the choice therefore matters little for size; the clear gains are over the residual score of BCCP and over stratified CQR.

## 6.6 Relevance-weighted coverage of the most extreme outcomes (RQ7)

Stratified calibration left the most extreme outcomes under-covered (top-1% coverage of SDLC 0.688 on the held-out datasets). RW-DLC was evaluated under its own pre-specified plan (Section 5.7); Table 9 reports the held-out datasets (17 datasets, 85 runs) and Supplementary Table S19 the CTR23 datasets.

* *RW-1 (validity).* The relevance-weighted coverage of RW-DLC was 0.898 on average, equivalent to the nominal 0.90 within the pre-specified margin of 0.02 (TOST $p$ < 0.001), as Theorem 2 predicts.
* *RW-2 (marginal methods miss the weighted target).* RW-DLC exceeded every marginal method in relevance-weighted coverage, by 0.122 (NCP) to 0.180 (DLS-M) on average (largest Holm-adjusted $p$ < 0.001).
* *RW-3 (extreme tail).* RW-DLC covered 0.872 of the top 1% of outcomes, against 0.688 for SDLC (mean difference 0.184, 95% CI 0.063–0.310, higher on 13 of 15 datasets with top-1% test points, one-sided $p$ = 0.011).
* *RW-4 (score).* RW-DLC's sets were smaller than those of the two ablations that use relevance-weighted calibration with an unweighted score, RW-DLS (geometric-mean ratio 0.885) and RW-NCP (0.904), but after Holm correction neither difference reached the pre-specified threshold ($p$ = 0.053 for both), so RW-4 was not supported.

The guarantee has a price elsewhere. Because RW-DLC averages coverage over the target range, it spends less on the bulk: its marginal coverage was 0.884 (minimum over datasets 0.750) and its worst coverage over target deciles was lower than that of SDLC (mean difference -0.080). Its sets were not reliably larger than SDLC's (geometric-mean ratio 1.081, 95% CI 0.916–1.240, descriptive). No test point had an infinite threshold; the smallest effective calibration sample size was 120 (median 1,368). The milder relevance $\phi_H$ gave intermediate results. RW-DLC and SDLC thus answer different requirements: SDLC guarantees every stratum, and RW-DLC guarantees an average over the whole target range that reaches the outcomes beyond the strata (Fig. 6).

## 6.7 Target shift (RQ4)

Table 10 and Fig. 7 report coverage under shift. Under the positive control, which re-weights whole strata, the stratum-wise coverage of every stratified method was unchanged, as Corollary 1(i) states. Their marginal coverage stayed at the nominal level (0.904 for SDLC), whereas that of the marginal methods fell to 0.838–0.877. Continuous tilts towards the upper tail also re-weight targets *within* strata, so Corollary 1(i) does not apply. Under a moderate tilt ($\gamma=1$) the stratified methods kept 0.861–0.881 coverage of the upper stratum, against 0.633–0.783 for the marginal methods. Under a strong tilt ($\gamma=2$) every method lost coverage. The stratified methods lost less (0.770–0.833, against 0.550–0.726). Among them, SDLC (0.798) was below S-NCP and S-HPD (0.833), which is the same reallocation away from the most extreme outcomes seen above. The effective sample size of the $\gamma=2$ weights was small (median 49, minimum 5.9). Restricting the analysis to the 15 datasets with an effective sample size of at least 50 left the ordering unchanged. The bounds of Corollary 1(ii)–(iii) are not informative for these unbounded tilts. The synthetic study (Section 6.4) adds a genuine label shift with known $X\,|\, Y$.

## 6.8 Tail alerts and decisions (RQ5)

A set that intersects the upper stratum raises an alert. Every stratified method guarantees an alert recall of at least 0.9; their mean recall was 0.978–0.988, and it was at least 0.9 on all 31 datasets (Table 11). The price of this conservativeness is a high alert rate. Among the stratified methods SDLC raised fewer alerts than BCCP (25/5 datasets) and S-CQR, but more than S-NCP (12/18). A dedicated conformal tail classifier built from the same location–scale model (TC-LS) attains the guaranteed recall almost exactly (mean 0.906) and raised fewer alerts than SDLC on every dataset (SDLC/TC-LS alert-rate ratio 1.559, SDLC lower on 0 of 31 datasets). For a pure yes/no decision about extremes, a dedicated classifier is therefore the more efficient tool. Conformal selection [@jin2023selection] controls the false discovery rate instead and flagged few units, with high precision (mean 0.766 at $q=0.2$) but a mean recall of only 0.603; the naive point-prediction rule behaved similarly (recall 0.625).

Which rule is preferable depends on the cost of a missed extreme outcome relative to a false alert (Table 11). With equal costs, the rules that raise few alerts were cheapest: the point rule and conformal selection (expected cost 0.056 and 0.062 per unit, against 0.277 for SDLC). When a miss costs ten times a false alert, SDLC's expected cost did not differ significantly from that of TC-LS (SDLC lower on 14 of 31 datasets, $p$ = 0.130) and was lower than that of conformal selection and of the point rule on 19 and 25 datasets. At a cost ratio of 20, SDLC was cheaper than TC-LS on 22 datasets ($p$ = 0.083), because its recall exceeds the guaranteed 0.9 while TC-LS attains it almost exactly. Among the stratified scores, however, S-NCP gave the cheapest alerts: SDLC's expected cost was higher than S-NCP's on 27 of 31 datasets at $r=10$ and on 29 at $r=20$, because S-NCP raised fewer upper-tail alerts. The $p$-values of the alert comparisons are unadjusted and descriptive. Alerts read off *marginal* sets (SCP, CQR, HPD-split) were the cheapest on average at high cost ratios (0.279 for HPD-split at $r=20$). They had no recall guarantee, however, and their recall fell below 0.9 on 4–6 datasets. Fig. 8 shows the cost curves for three valuation problems (house prices in King County and Miami, wages in the CPS). The guarantee-free point rule and conformal selection become the most expensive rules as the cost ratio grows, and set-based alerts with stratum-wise coverage are the cheapest at the highest ratios. A set also carries more information than an alert: it says how large the outcome may be, which a downstream decision such as a provision, an inspection priority or a price range can use.

## 6.9 Sensitivity analyses (RQ6)

*Miscoverage level and strata.* The conclusions held at $\alpha=0.05$ and $\alpha=0.2$ (Supplementary Table S6): stratified methods restored worst-stratum coverage to 0.947 and 0.789, and SDLC had the smallest stratified sets. Finer strata protected narrower ranges of the target: with decile strata (S10) the worst decile coverage of SDLC rose to 0.888, from 0.863 with five strata, at a size increase from 0.874 to 0.898. The floor variant (SDLC-floor, $\beta=0.2$) reached its weaker guarantee with smaller sets (worst stratum 0.821, size 0.806).

*Calibration-set size.* Stratified calibration needs enough calibration points in every stratum (Supplementary Fig. S3, Table S8). With a quarter of the calibration split (on average 1,082 points) stratum-wise validity was retained, but the stratified sets grew from 0.874 to 1.243 for SDLC. The main reason was that a tail stratum with fewer than $1/\alpha-1$ points has an infinite threshold, which happened in 87 runs, against 2 with the full split. Marginal methods are insensitive to this, and for small datasets stratified calibration should be combined with fewer strata or a lower stratum level $\beta$ (the floor variant).

*Density estimator.* Among the density estimators, the symmetric location–scale density was as efficient as the two-piece variant and much more efficient than the histogram density (Table 6); the defensive $t$ component and the cross-fitted standardisation are part of every result reported here.

*Base learner.* With a two-layer perceptron as point and scale model (same pipeline, 5 seeds), the coverage results were unchanged: marginal methods reached a worst-stratum coverage of 0.650–0.830, stratified methods 0.889–0.891. SDLC again gave the smallest stratified sets: smaller than BCCP on 23 of 31 datasets (ratio 0.946, Holm $p$ = 0.019), than S-NCP on 27 (ratio 0.938, $p$ < 0.001) and than S-HPD on 29 (ratio 0.927, $p$ < 0.001). The gain over S-NCP was larger than with gradient boosting.

*Imbalance-aware training.* Does a base learner trained for imbalanced regression remove the need for stratified calibration? With DenseWeight sample weights [@steininger2021denseweight] (seeds 0–2), the marginal methods covered the tails better than with unweighted training on the same splits: SCP's worst-stratum coverage rose from 0.630 to 0.759, and CQR's from 0.720 to 0.775. The shortfall remained large, however, and the sets grew by factors of 1.31 (SCP) and 1.45 (CQR). Stratified calibration remained valid with the weighted learner (0.885 for SDLC), and SDLC again gave the smallest stratified sets (ratios 0.906, 0.948, 0.694 and 0.964 against BCCP, S-NCP, S-CQR and S-HPD; Holm $p$ < 0.001 or smaller). Imbalance-aware point learning and stratified calibration are thus complementary, and the former does not deliver the coverage guarantee.

*Grouped splits.* Three datasets contain repeated units that random splits can place in both the training and the test set: exact duplicate rows (sarcos), identical feature vectors with different targets (superconductivity) and many transcoding runs of the same input video (video_transcoding). We repeated the main comparison on these datasets with splits that keep every unit, feature vector or video in a single part (5 seeds; Supplementary Table S16). On sarcos and superconductivity the results were close to those of random splits: SDLC's worst-stratum coverage was 0.891 and 0.890, and its sets were smaller than those of S-NCP (ratios 0.965 and 0.914) and BCCP (0.889 and 0.823). On video_transcoding, splitting by video increased the set sizes of every method several-fold (SDLC: 0.113 to 0.775 in units of the target standard deviation), which shows that random splits of this dataset measure interpolation between runs of known videos. Stratified methods still covered their worst stratum at 0.852–0.904, against at most 0.766 for marginal methods, but under grouped splits SDLC's sets were larger than BCCP's (ratio 1.205) while remaining smaller than S-NCP's (0.882). With only five seeds and few groups per stratum, BCCP and S-CQR also covered less than nominal marginally (0.875 and 0.873), as expected when exchangeability holds between groups rather than rows. The size advantage of SDLC therefore depends on the density model transferring to unseen units, and grouped validation is advisable when data contain repeated units.

*Computational cost.* Fitting SDLC's density model took a median of 0.31 s for the mean model, 1.01 s for the scale model with out-of-fold residuals, 0.40 s for the cross-fitted scale models and 0.004 s for the kernel density over the 31 datasets on one CPU core (Supplementary Table S15). Computing a prediction set took a median of 13.5 µs per test point for SDLC, against 0.09 µs for S-NCP, 0.07 µs for BCCP and 12.4 µs for S-HPD (Supplementary Table S13). SDLC is thus roughly two orders of magnitude slower than interval-based stratified methods at prediction time, but at tens of microseconds per point the cost is negligible next to model fitting in tabular applications.

## 6.10 Error analysis

Where does stratification cost the most, and where does SDLC lose to simpler scores? The price of stratum-wise validity (SDLC relative to DLS) rose with the skewness of the target (Spearman $\rho$ = 0.62, $p$ < 0.001) and fell with the calibration-set size ($\rho$ = -0.41, $p$ = 0.022); it was weakly related to the predictability of the target ($\rho$ = 0.38, $p$ = 0.034). The largest price occurred for forest fires (8.75): the burned area is zero for 47.8% of the fires, the atom at zero merges the two lower strata, and covering the heavy upper tail of an almost unpredictable target requires very long sets. # 7. Discussion

## 7.1 What the evidence supports

Three findings are robust across datasets, miscoverage levels, stratifications and learner families. First, marginal and covariate-adaptive conformal methods systematically under-cover rare targets. Their worst-stratum coverage at a nominal 90% was 63.0%–78.3% on average, and the null reference shows that this is not sampling noise. The oracle analysis (Section 3) and the synthetic study show that the shortfall is not an artefact of estimation, although its size depends on the data-generating process. Second, calibrating within target strata restores stratum-wise coverage for every score, as Mondrian theory guarantees and as BCCP [@randahl2026bin] showed in simulations and one application. In our benchmark the guarantee held across 31 datasets, resisted moderate target shift, and cost a geometric-mean size increase of 1.17–1.27-fold. It is also the efficient way to protect the tails: lowering $\alpha$ for a marginal method bought less tail coverage at a larger size. Third, the score matters for efficiency in the direction predicted by Proposition 2. The density score gave smaller stratum-valid sets than the residual score of BCCP, the quantile score and the HPD score on most datasets, and the synthetic study confirms the ordering with known densities. The size ordering was confirmed on 17 held-out datasets under a pre-specified analysis plan.

Two qualifications are equally well supported. The gain of the density score over the information-matched normalised residual is small, a few per cent, and it comes mainly from the factor $1/\hat\sigma(x)$, that is, from reallocating coverage from noisy to quiet inputs. Its price is lower coverage of the noisiest inputs, occasional empty sets and lower coverage of the most extreme 1% of outcomes, which is not calibrated. Moreover, the estimated location–scale density is far from the oracle when the noise shape changes with $x$: in the bimodal synthetic design the oracle density set was much smaller than any estimated stratified set. The ranking of scores is therefore a statement about efficiency on average, not a free improvement.

## 7.2 Practical guidance

For practitioners who must report how often extreme outcomes are covered, the results suggest the following.

1. *Decide which guarantee is required.* Marginal coverage, however adaptive the score, does not protect rare targets, and lowering $\alpha$ is a less efficient substitute. If every target range must be covered at the nominal level, calibrate within strata (SDLC or S-NCP). If the requirement is coverage averaged over the target range, or if the most extreme outcomes beyond any practical stratum matter most, use relevance-weighted calibration (RW-DLC) with an inverse-density relevance; it covered the top 1% at close to the nominal level, at the price of lower coverage in the bulk.
2. *Choose strata from training-target quantiles* so that each stratum receives at least several times $1/\alpha$ calibration points; otherwise the method returns whole strata. Finer strata extend protection to narrower target ranges at a modest cost in size. With small calibration sets, use fewer strata or the floor variant.
3. *Use a score that includes the scale $\hat\sigma(x)$.* SDLC, S-NCP and S-HPD gave stratum-valid sets within a few per cent of each other; SDLC was smallest on most datasets, while S-NCP is simpler, spreads coverage more evenly across noise levels, covers more of the extreme 1% and gives cheaper alerts. The residual score of BCCP and stratified CQR were clearly less efficient. When an interval is required, the hull of S-NCP was as small as the hull of SDLC on held-out data.
4. *Report hulls when an interval is mandatory.* Stratified sets can be discontiguous: SDLC's sets had 1.527 pieces on average (BCCP 1.465), mostly an isolated piece in a tail stratum whose threshold is lower than that of its neighbour (Fig. 9). Such a piece is informative, because it says that an extreme outcome is plausible, but an interval is easier to communicate. SDLC-hull is valid and remains smaller than BCCP-hull, but it loses part of the advantage.
5. *For yes/no decisions about extremes, match the rule to the costs.* A class-conditional conformal tail classifier attains the same recall guarantee with fewer alerts and is the better choice when false alerts are expensive; among set-based alerts, S-NCP's were the cheapest. Set-based alerts have higher recall, are cheaper when misses are very costly, and carry more information about the size of the outcome.

## 7.3 Limitations

*Size cost and data requirements.* Stratum-wise validity is paid for in set size, and the payment grows with skewness and when tail strata are small. With a quarter of the calibration set, sets became considerably larger, and with fewer than $1/\alpha_k-1$ calibration points the method returns the whole stratum. Pooling sparse strata, as in clustered conformal prediction [@ding2023class], cross-fitted calibration [@barber2021jackknife] and overlapping or weighted groups as in Kandinsky conformal prediction [@bairaktari2025kandinsky] are natural remedies that we did not evaluate. Any pooling rule must be decided from training data only to preserve validity.

*Granularity of the guarantee.* The guarantee holds for the calibrated strata, not for arbitrary sub-ranges. Within a stratum, coverage is lower near its outer end, and shifts that move mass within a stratum reduce coverage (Table 10); Corollary 1 explains the direction of this effect, but its bounds are loose for open tail strata. The guarantee is also marginal over $X$ within each stratum, so it says nothing about a particular input, and the density score deliberately trades coverage across inputs.

*Density estimation and baselines.* The optimality of Proposition 2 is an oracle property, and Proposition 3 requires a uniformly consistent density estimate, which the location–scale model provides only when the noise shape does not depend on $x$. Flexible conditional density estimators (mixture density networks, normalising flows, distributional boosting) may close the gap to the oracle. We did not run several covariate-adaptive methods that could be stratified in the same way, such as conditional histograms [@sesia2021conformal], conditional calibration [@gibbs2025conformal] or localised conformal prediction [@hore2025local]; Proposition 2 implies that none of them can beat the oracle density score, but their estimated versions may be competitive.

*Evaluation scope.* The benchmark covers tabular data with targets having at least 20 distinct values; ordinal, count and temporally dependent targets were excluded. Several datasets contain repeated units (duplicate rows in sarcos, repeated videos, repeat sales, team-season panels). Random splitting keeps the rows exchangeable, so the guarantees hold for new rows from the same pool, but not for new units or future periods. All results are for split calibration with a 50/25/25 split and one fixed configuration of the base learners, chosen without tuning to avoid favouring any method. The implementation of the density-based methods changed after earlier results had been seen (Section 5.7); this is why the size comparisons on CTR23 are labelled exploratory and were replicated on held-out data.

*Applicability of the guarantee.* Like all conformal guarantees, stratum-wise coverage requires exchangeability of calibration and test data. It holds on average over calibration sets and over the test points of a stratum, and it does not protect against shifts of the covariate distribution within strata.


# 8. Conclusion and future work

Prediction intervals that are valid on average can fail exactly where imbalanced regression needs them: on the rare outcomes. Even the oracle interval with exact covariate-conditional coverage under-covers the tails of the target, and on 31 benchmark datasets marginal and covariate-adaptive conformal methods covered only 63.0%–78.3% of the outcomes in their worst-covered stratum at a nominal 90%. We studied two guarantees that protect rare outcomes and the sets that meet them most efficiently.

Calibrating thresholds within strata of the target, an established Mondrian device, guarantees coverage in every stratum for any score. For this guarantee, conditional-density level sets with stratum-specific thresholds are the smallest sets for the oracle, and SDLC, their plug-in version, gave smaller sets than bin-conditional conformal prediction with residual scores and than stratified quantile regression, on most CTR23 datasets and on 17 held-out datasets under a pre-specified analysis. Among scores that use the estimated scale, however, the differences were within a few per cent, and the simpler stratified normalised residual covered noisy inputs and the most extreme outcomes better. Strata also protect only at their own resolution. Relevance-weighted calibration removes that limit: RW-DLC guarantees coverage averaged by an imbalanced-regression relevance function in finite samples, uses the score that is size-optimal for that guarantee, and in a second pre-specified study covered 87.2% of the top 1% of outcomes on held-out data, against 68.8% for SDLC, at a cost in coverage of the bulk. For yes/no decisions about extremes, dedicated conformal tail classifiers attain the same recall guarantee with fewer alerts.

Future work should combine the two guarantees, for example a relevance-weighted guarantee with a floor on every stratum; learn relevance functions or strata that minimise size subject to validity; replace the location–scale density by flexible conditional density estimators, whose gap to the oracle was largest under covariate-dependent multimodality; and extend both guarantees to grouped and dependent data, where our grouped-split analysis showed that efficiency depends on transfer to unseen units.

**Proof of Theorem 1.** Condition on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$, which fixes the score $s$ and the strata. Let $E_k=\{Y_{n+1}\in B_k\}$ and $J=\{i\le n+1: Y_i\in B_k\}$, so that $|J|=n_k+1$ on $E_k$. Membership of a point in $B_k$ is a function of that point alone, so any permutation of $\{1,\ldots,n+1\}$ that maps $J$ onto itself leaves the event $\{J=j,\ n+1\in J\}$ unchanged; by exchangeability, conditionally on $J=j\ni n+1$ and on the multiset $\{(X_i,Y_i):i\in j\}$, the index $n+1$ is uniformly distributed over $j$. Let $R$ be the rank of $S_{n+1}=s(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})$ among $\{S_i\}_{i\in J}$, with ties broken uniformly at random; conditionally as above, $R$ is uniform on $\{1,\ldots,n_k+1\}$. By (3)–(4), $Y_{n+1}\in\hat C(X_{n+1})$ iff $S_{n+1}\le\hat q_k$, which holds whenever $R\le r_k=\lceil(n_k+1)(1-\alpha_k)\rceil$ (if $r_k>n_k$, then $\hat q_k=+\infty$ and coverage is one). Hence the conditional coverage is at least $r_k/(n_k+1)\ge 1-\alpha_k$. Averaging over $J$ and the multiset gives (5); since the argument conditions on $J$, the level $\alpha_k$ may depend on $n_k$. If scores are almost surely distinct, coverage is exactly $\min\{r_k,n_k+1\}/(n_k+1)<1-\alpha_k+1/(n_k+1)$ given $n_k$. The marginal statement follows from the law of total probability, $\sum_kp_k(1-\alpha_k)=1-\sum_kp_k\alpha_k$. Finally, the conditioning on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ allows any score and strata that are measurable functions of $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ alone. $\square$

**Proof of Corollary 1.** Given $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$ the set $A=\{(x,y):y\in\hat C(x)\}$ is fixed. Because the test point is independent of the calibration data, $Q\{Y\in\hat C(X)\,|\, Y\in B_k\}=\mathbb{E}\,Q_k(A)$ and the left-hand side of (5) equals $\mathbb{E}\,P_k(A)$, where $\mathbb{E}$ averages over $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}\sim P^n$ (i.i.d. data are exchangeable, so (5) applies). (i) If $Q_k=P_k$ the two expectations coincide, and the law of total probability gives the marginal statement. (ii) $|Q_k(A)-P_k(A)|\le d_{\operatorname{TV}}(Q_k,P_k)$ for every fixed $A$; average and use (5). (iii) Under label shift the likelihood ratio of $Q_k$ to $P_k$ is $\lambda_k(y)=w(y)/\bar w_k$ with $\bar w_k=\pi_k/p_k=\mathbb{E}_{P_k}w(Y)\ge m_k$. Hence $Q_k(A^c)=\mathbb{E}_{P_k}\{\lambda_k(Y)\mathbf{1}_{A^c}(X,Y)\}\le(M_k/\bar w_k)P_k(A^c)\le(M_k/m_k)P_k(A^c)$; averaging over $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$ and using (5) gives the multiplicative bound. For the total-variation bound, $\lambda_k\in[a,b]$ with $a=m_k/\bar w_k\le1\le b=M_k/\bar w_k$ and $\mathbb{E}_{P_k}\lambda_k=1$; since $d_{\operatorname{TV}}(Q_k,P_k)=\mathbb{E}_{P_k}(\lambda_k-1)_+$ and, by convexity, $(\lambda-1)_+\le(b-1)(\lambda-a)/(b-a)$ on $[a,b]$, we obtain $d_{\operatorname{TV}}\le(b-1)(1-a)/(b-a)=(M_k-\bar w_k)(\bar w_k-m_k)/\{\bar w_k(M_k-m_k)\}$. The right-hand side is maximised at $\bar w_k=\sqrt{m_kM_k}$, where it equals $(\sqrt{M_k}-\sqrt{m_k})/(\sqrt{M_k}+\sqrt{m_k})=\tanh\{\log(M_k/m_k)/4\}$. $\square$

**Proof of Proposition 2.** Existence: $G_k(t)=P\{f(Y\,|\, X)\ge t\,|\, Y\in B_k\}$ is continuous and non-increasing with $G_k(0^+)=1$ and $G_k(t)\to0$ as $t\to\infty$, so the intermediate value theorem gives $t_k>0$ with $G_k(t_k)=1-\alpha_k$. Optimality: write $A=\{(x,y):y\in C(x)\}$, $A^\star$ likewise, $A_k=A\cap(\mathcal{X}\times B_k)$, $A_k^\star=A^\star\cap(\mathcal{X}\times B_k)$ and $|A|_P=\iint\mathbf{1}_A(x,y)\,dy\,P_X(dx)$, so that $\mathbb{E}|C(X)\cap B_k|=|A_k|_P$ and $p_k\operatorname{Cov}_k(C)=\iint\mathbf{1}_{A_k}f(y\,|\, x)\,dy\,P_X(dx)$. Since $|\{y:f(y\,|\, x)\ge t_k\}|\le 1/t_k$, $|A_k^\star|_P<\infty$; if $|A_k|_P=\infty$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise the integrand of
$$\Delta_k=\iint\left(\mathbf{1}_{A_k^\star}-\mathbf{1}_{A_k}\right)\left(f(y\,|\, x)-t_k\right)dy\,P_X(dx)$$
is non-negative pointwise: on $A_k^\star\setminus A_k$ we have $f\ge t_k$, and on $A_k\setminus A_k^\star$, where $y\in B_k$, we have $f<t_k$. Hence $\Delta_k\ge 0$, i.e. $t_k(|A_k^\star|_P-|A_k|_P)\le p_k\{\operatorname{Cov}_k(C^\star)-\operatorname{Cov}_k(C)\}\le 0$, and $t_k>0$ gives $|A_k^\star|_P\le|A_k|_P$. Summing over $k$ gives the statement for $\mathbb{E}|C(X)|$. $\square$

**Proof of Proposition 3.** Condition on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ (hence on $\hat f$) and write $V_i=f(Y_i\,|\, X_i)$, $\hat V_i=\hat f(Y_i\,|\, X_i)$, so that $|\hat V_i-V_i|\le\varepsilon_N$. *Thresholds.* By (3), $-\hat q_k$ is the $(n_k+1-r_k)$-th smallest of $\{\hat V_i:i\in I_k\}$, and $(n_k+1-r_k)/n_k\to\alpha_k$ because $n_k\to\infty$ almost surely ($n_k\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p_k)$ given $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$). Order statistics are 1-Lipschitz in the sup-norm, so $-\hat q_k$ differs by at most $\varepsilon_N$ from the same order statistic of $\{V_i:i\in I_k\}$. The $V_i$, $i\in I_k$, are i.i.d. from the law of $f(Y\,|\, X)$ given $Y\in B_k$, independent of $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$, whose $\alpha_k$-quantile $t_k$ is unique by assumption; hence that order statistic converges to $t_k$ almost surely [@serfling1980approximation, Sec. 2.3], and $\delta_k:=|\hat q_k+t_k|\to0$ in probability. *Sets.* Fix $x$ and $y\in B_k$. If $y$ lies in exactly one of $\hat C(x)$ and $C^\star(x)$, then $\hat f(y\,|\, x)+\hat q_k$ and $f(y\,|\, x)-t_k$ have opposite signs (one is $\ge0$, the other $<0$), while they differ by at most $\varepsilon_N+\delta_k$; hence $|f(y\,|\, x)-t_k|\le\eta:=\varepsilon_N+\max_k\delta_k$. Therefore
$$\mathbb{E}_X|\hat C(X)\triangle C^\star(X)|\le\sum_{k=1}^K h_k(\eta),\qquad h_k(\eta)=\mathbb{E}\,\big|\{y\in B_k:|f(y\,|\, X)-t_k|\le\eta\}\big|.$$
For $\eta<t_k/2$ the set inside $h_k$ lies in $\{y:f(y\,|\, X)\ge t_k/2\}$, whose measure is at most $2/t_k$, and it decreases to $\{y\in B_k:f(y\,|\, X)=t_k\}$ as $\eta\downarrow0$. By dominated convergence $h_k(\eta)\to\mathbb{E}|\{y\in B_k:f(y\,|\, X)=t_k\}|$, which is zero: continuity of the law of $f(Y\,|\, X)$ given $Y\in B_k$ gives $0=p_k^{-1}\iint_{B_k}\mathbf{1}\{f(y\,|\, x)=t_k\}f(y\,|\, x)\,dy\,P_X(dx)=p_k^{-1}t_k\,\mathbb{E}|\{y\in B_k:f(y\,|\, X)=t_k\}|$. Since $\eta\to0$ in probability, (7) follows, and $|\,|\hat C|-|C^\star|\,|\le|\hat C\triangle C^\star|$ gives the statement on expected size. $\square$

**Remark (within-stratum coverage profile).** Under label shift, let $\kappa(y)=P\{y\in\hat C(X_{n+1})\,|\, Y_{n+1}=y\}$, which is the same under $P$ and $Q$. Then the change in stratum-wise coverage equals the within-stratum covariance $\mathbb{E}_{P_k}[\{\kappa(Y)-c\}\{\lambda_k(Y)-1\}]$ for any constant $c$, so coverage is lost only if the shift moves mass within $B_k$ towards values that are covered less often than the stratum average. For the oracle sets $C^\star$ in the Gaussian model of Proposition 1, $C^\star(x)\cap B_k=\{y\in B_k:|y-x|\le r_k\}$ for a stratum-specific radius $r_k$, so the argument of Proposition 1 (with $a=r_k$) shows that $\kappa$ decreases in $|y|$ within every stratum, that is, towards the outer end of a tail stratum; for the estimated sets the same pattern is observed empirically (Section 6). This is why a tilt towards more extreme outcomes can lower tail-stratum coverage even after stratified calibration.

**Proof of Theorem 2.** Write $Z_i=(X_i,Y_i)$ and let $Z_{n+1}=(X,Y)$ be drawn from $Q$ with $dQ/dP(z)=w(z)=\phi(y)/\mathbb{E}\phi(Y)$, independently of $Z_1,\ldots,Z_n\sim P$; conditionally on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ the score $s$ and $\phi$ are fixed. The joint law of $(Z_1,\ldots,Z_{n+1})$ has density $\prod_{i\le n}p(z_i)\cdot p(z_{n+1})w(z_{n+1})$ with respect to the product of the dominating measures, so the vector is weighted exchangeable with weight functions $(1,\ldots,1,w)$ [@tibshirani2019conformal, Lemma 3]. Hence, conditionally on the multiset $E=\{z_1,\ldots,z_{n+1}\}$ of observed values, $P(Z_{n+1}=z_j\,|\, E)=w(z_j)/\sum_kw(z_k)=\phi(y_j)/\sum_k\phi(y_k)$, and the test score $S_{n+1}$ is distributed as $\sum_jp_j\delta_{S_j}$ with $p_j=\phi(y_j)/\sum_k\phi(y_k)$. Therefore $P\{S_{n+1}\le Q_{1-\alpha}(\sum_{j\le n+1}p_j\delta_{S_j})\,|\, E\}\ge1-\alpha$, where $Q_{1-\alpha}$ denotes the $(1-\alpha)$-quantile. Replacing the atom $\delta_{S_{n+1}}$ by $\delta_{+\infty}$ can only raise the quantile, and $S_{n+1}\le Q_{1-\alpha}(\sum_{j\le n+1}p_j\delta_{S_j})$ if and only if $S_{n+1}\le Q_{1-\alpha}(\sum_{i\le n}p_i\delta_{S_i}+p_{n+1}\delta_{+\infty})$ [@tibshirani2019conformal, Lemma 1]. The right-hand quantile equals $q(Y_{n+1})$ in (11), because the normalisation $\sum_k\phi(y_k)=W+\phi(Y_{n+1})$ is the same. Taking expectations over $E$ gives $Q\{Y\in\hat C_\phi(X)\}\ge1-\alpha$. Finally, for a fixed set $C$, $Q\{Y\in C(X)\}=\mathbb{E}_P[w(Z)\mathbf{1}\{Y\in C(X)\}]=\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(C)$, so averaging over $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$ yields $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(\hat C_\phi)]\ge1-\alpha$. $\square$

**Proof of Proposition 4.** Let $C$ satisfy $\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(C)\ge1-\alpha=\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(C^\star_\phi)$ and write $h(x,y)=\phi(y)f(y\,|\, x)$. Pointwise, $(\mathbf{1}\{y\in C^\star_\phi(x)\}-\mathbf{1}\{y\in C(x)\})(h(x,y)-t)\ge0$, because the first factor is positive only where $h\ge t$ and negative only where $h<t$. Integrating over $dy\,dP_X(x)$ gives $\mathbb{E}[\phi(Y)](\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(C^\star_\phi)-\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(C))\ge t\,(\mathbb{E}|C^\star_\phi(X)|-\mathbb{E}|C(X)|)$. The left-hand side is at most zero and $t>0$, so $\mathbb{E}|C^\star_\phi(X)|\le\mathbb{E}|C(X)|$. $\square$
