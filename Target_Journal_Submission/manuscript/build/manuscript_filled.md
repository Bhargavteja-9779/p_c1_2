abstract: |

# 1. Introduction

Many regression problems that intelligent systems solve are judged by how they handle *rare* outcomes: the unusually expensive property, the exceptional salary, the extreme load on a power grid, the very toxic compound. Rare outcomes are under-represented in training data, and models trained by minimising average loss shrink their predictions towards the bulk of the target distribution. This is the setting of *imbalanced regression* [@branco2016survey; @ribeiro2020imbalanced; @yang2021delving], where a growing literature re-weights, re-samples or re-parameterises models so that point predictions for rare targets become more accurate.

Point predictions are only half of what a decision maker needs. Prediction *sets* with a coverage guarantee state how far the truth may be from the prediction, and conformal prediction delivers such guarantees in finite samples for any model, under exchangeability only [@vovk2005alrw; @lei2018distribution; @angelopoulos2023gentle]. Its standard guarantee, however, is *marginal*: the set contains the outcome with probability $1-\alpha$ *on average over all outcomes*. When rare outcomes matter, the relevant question is different: **when the outcome is extreme, how often does the set contain it?** A marginal guarantee is silent on this, because the frequent bulk can be over-covered while the rare tails are under-covered.

Much work makes conformal sets adapt to the covariates, so that coverage holds approximately conditionally on $X$ [@romano2019conformalized; @chernozhukov2021distributional; @izbicki2022cdsplit; @gibbs2025conformal]. Such sets do not protect rare outcomes, and better estimation would not change this: even the *oracle* set with exact coverage $1-\alpha$ at every $x$ under-covers the tails, because an extreme outcome typically combines a large signal with a large, unpredictable noise realisation that no $X$-measurable set can anticipate. Coverage *conditional on the target*, which we call stratum-wise coverage, must be targeted explicitly.

Calibrating conformal thresholds separately within bins of the observed outcome is the natural device, and it is not new. @randahl2026bin proposed it as bin-conditional conformal prediction (BCCP) for conflict fatalities, and @wang2023equal calibrate within outcome bins to equalise coverage across groups. Its bin-wise validity is an instance of Mondrian (label-conditional) validity [@vovk2005alrw; @vovk2013conditional]. Two questions remain open. First, the guarantee holds for any score, so efficiency is decided by the score, and it is not known which score makes stratum-valid sets smallest or what that efficiency costs. Second, strata protect rare outcomes only at their own resolution: the most extreme outcomes lie inside a tail stratum and are covered only on average with it, while finer strata leave too few calibration points. Imbalanced regression usually describes the importance of an outcome by a continuous *relevance function* [@torgo2007utility; @ribeiro2020imbalanced; @steininger2021denseweight], and long-tailed classification has obtained conformal guarantees that average coverage over classes [@ding2025longtailed; @bhattacharyya2026macro]. We address both questions and, above all, what a practitioner should do. Our contributions are four.

1. **Diagnosis.** Standard and covariate-adaptive conformal intervals under-cover rare outcomes on almost every dataset, and so does the oracle covariate-conditional interval (Proposition 1: 70.5% of the top decile in a Gaussian model with half of the variance explained), so better models cannot remove the shortfall.
2. **The price of tail validity.** Calibrating within target strata restores coverage in every stratum for any score at a size cost of about 1.24, smaller than that of lowering the miscoverage level for all outcomes, and remains exact when the shift re-weights whole strata.
3. **Size-optimal scores for two tail guarantees.** Conditional-density level sets with stratum-specific thresholds are the smallest stratum-valid sets (Proposition 2, the per-stratum form of known level-set results [@lei2013distribution; @sadinle2019least]); SDLC is their plug-in method. For *relevance-weighted coverage*, the average of coverage weighted by a relevance function, relevance-weighted calibration is valid in finite samples (Theorem 2, weighted conformal prediction with known weights) and level sets of relevance times density are size-optimal (Proposition 4); RW-DLC implements them. Its sets resemble those of conformal prediction under an estimated target shift [@yi2025distribution], and relevance-weighted coverage is a single-weight member of the coverage families of Kandinsky conformal prediction [@bairaktari2025kandinsky]; what is new is the criterion for imbalanced regression and its evaluation. Empirically, among scores that use the estimated scale the choice changes set sizes by only a few percent, and we report this as a finding.
4. **A decision guide.** We compare which guarantee to choose, how to choose strata, sets versus intervals, and set-based alerts versus conformal tail classifiers and conformal selection [@jin2023selection] under explicit costs (Section 7).

Both methods decide coverage exactly at the observed target and compute set geometry on a fine grid, at a cost linear in the number of test points. The evidence comes from all 31 eligible OpenML-CTR23 datasets with 10 predefined splits, where we compare the stratified methods with BCCP, stratified normalised-residual, quantile and HPD scores and seven marginal or covariate-adaptive methods, with ablations of each score component, and from 17 held-out datasets analysed under two pre-specified plans. We also analyse robustness to target shift, imbalance-aware training, a second learner family, and grouped and temporal splits.

Section 2 reviews related work, Section 3 formalises the problem, Section 4 presents SDLC and RW-DLC, Sections 5 and 6 describe the experiments and their results, and Sections 7 and 8 discuss them and conclude.


# 2. Related work

Four lines of work meet in this paper: covariate-adaptive conformal regression, calibration within label-defined categories, imbalanced regression, and conformal prediction under distribution shift.

## 2.1 Conformal regression and covariate-conditional coverage

Split conformal prediction calibrates a nonconformity score of any fitted regressor on held-out data and guarantees marginal coverage in finite samples under exchangeability [@papadopoulos2002inductive; @vovk2005alrw; @lei2018distribution]. Much subsequent work changes the score so that sets adapt to the covariates: normalised residuals [@lei2018distribution], conformalised quantile regression (CQR) [@romano2019conformalized; @sesia2020comparison], distributional conformal prediction (DCP) [@chernozhukov2021distributional], conformal predictive distributions [@vovk2019nonparametric] and jackknife+ [@barber2021jackknife]. A family of methods builds sets from density estimates: conformal density level sets [@lei2013distribution; @lei2014bands], Dist-split and CD-split [@izbicki2020flexible], HPD-split [@izbicki2022cdsplit], conditional histograms [@sesia2021conformal], regression-as-classification [@guha2024conformal] and conformal thresholded intervals [@luo2025cti]. Under coverage constraints indexed by covariate shifts, length-optimal sets are conditional-density level sets with a covariate-dependent level [@kiyani2024length]. SDLC uses a score from this family but calibrates it with stratum-specific thresholds.

These methods target approximate covariate-conditional coverage, which cannot be guaranteed in finite samples without further assumptions [@barber2021limits]. Relaxations include conditional calibration over classes of covariate groups or shifts [@gibbs2025conformal], localised calibration [@guan2023localized; @hore2025local], calibration within partitions learned by trees [@cabezas2025regression; @santos2026loboost], and learned scores that reduce conditional miscoverage [@xie2024boosted; @gao2024adjusting]. In intelligent systems, conformal prediction serves as an uncertainty layer, for example for option prices [@bastos2024conformal], tropical-cyclone tracks [@meng2024uncertainty] and uncertainty-aware deep-forest classifiers [@zhang2025conformal]. All these approaches condition, at most, on functions of $X$, and Proposition 1 shows that even exact covariate-conditional coverage leaves extreme outcomes under-covered. Covariate-adaptive scores are therefore complementary to stratified calibration, not a substitute.

## 2.2 Category-, label- and bin-conditional calibration

Mondrian conformal predictors [@vovk2005alrw; @vovk2022alrw] assign each example to a category that may depend on its label and calibrate within each category, which yields finite-sample category-conditional and, in particular, label-conditional validity [@vovk2013conditional; @toccaceli2019combination; @angelopoulos2024theoretical]. In classification, clustered conformal prediction pools classes with similar score distributions [@ding2023class], a device analogous to merging sparse strata. Closest to our optimality result is @sadinle2019least, who showed that the least ambiguous set-valued classifier under class-specific coverage constraints thresholds $p(y\,|\, x)$ at class-specific cut-offs [see also @lei2014classification; @chzhen2021setvalued]; Proposition 2 is the same characterisation for a continuous outcome. For long-tailed classification, @ding2025longtailed and @liu2025longtail interpolate between marginal and class-conditional coverage, and @bhattacharyya2026macro show that label-weighted conformal prediction guarantees macro-coverage and characterise its smallest sets. RW-DLC carries this idea to continuous outcomes, with a relevance function in place of class weights (Section 4.7). Kandinsky conformal prediction [@bairaktari2025kandinsky] covers overlapping or fractional groups defined jointly on covariates and labels, with a high-probability guarantee; disjoint target strata are its simplest case, for which Mondrian calibration is exact.

In regression, Mondrian categories have mostly been functions of $X$ or of the fitted model: binned difficulty estimates [@bostrom2020mondrian], regression trees [@johansson2018interpretable], covariate clusters [@sousa2024improving] and quartiles of point predictions [@boruah2026distribution], which reports under-coverage of the highest-strength quartile for steel fatigue; worst-bin coverage statistics are biased downwards [@boruah2026null]. Such guarantees do not extend to coverage conditional on $Y$.

Two regression methods calibrate within bins of the outcome itself. @wang2023equal require coverage to be equal across groups of a sensitive attribute conditionally on the outcome; their binned fair quantile regression (BFQR) calibrates within each group and equal-mass outcome bin at bin-specific levels chosen to minimise width, so coverage is equalised across groups but traded across bins. The closest work is BCCP [@randahl2026bin], which runs split conformal prediction with a residual score within user-defined outcome bins and returns the union of the per-bin sets or its hull; it guarantees coverage within each bin. Our stratified calibration step (Eqs. 3–4) coincides with BCCP when the absolute-residual score is used, and its validity (Theorem 1) is not claimed as new. BCCP leaves open which score yields the smallest bin-valid sets, where the efficiency comes from, and how the guarantee behaves under label shift; these are the questions we address.

## 2.3 Imbalanced regression and its uncertainty

Imbalanced regression concerns problems in which the most important targets are rare [@branco2016survey; @ribeiro2020imbalanced]. Utility-based regression encodes their importance through relevance functions [@torgo2007utility], and precision, recall [@torgo2009precision] and SERA [@ribeiro2020imbalanced] evaluate point predictions on the relevant range. Data-level methods synthesise rare examples, such as SMOTER [@torgo2013smote], SMOGN [@branco2017smogn] and their successors [@avelino2024resampling; @belhaouari2024oversampling; @alahyari2026smogan; @shahbazi2026hybrid]. Algorithm-level methods re-weight the loss, for example DenseWeight [@steininger2021denseweight], label distribution smoothing [@yang2021delving] and Balanced MSE [@ren2022balanced].

Uncertainty has entered this literature mainly as a means to better point predictions: quantile ensembles [@zhu2026swarm], variational imbalanced regression [@wang2023variational] and UQDIR [@dolar2025uncertainty] give no coverage guarantee, and extreme conformal prediction [@pasche2026extreme] addresses very high confidence levels instead. Guarantees on how often sets contain rare targets have so far come from conflict forecasting and fair regression [@randahl2026bin; @wang2023equal]. For yes/no decisions about extremes, conformal selection [@jin2023selection] and class-conditional conformal tail classifiers exist; we use both as baselines.

## 2.4 Distribution shift

Weighted conformal prediction handles covariate shift with likelihood-ratio weights [@tibshirani2019conformal]. Under label (target) shift, the law of $Y$ changes while that of $X$ given $Y$ does not. Re-weighting approaches for classification [@podkopaev2021distribution] and regression [@yi2025distribution; @lee2026split] estimate the shift, and exact validity is lost when it is misspecified [@choi2026continuous]. Label-conditional calibration needs no estimate, because a label shift re-weights whole classes [@podkopaev2021distribution]. Stratified calibration carries this property over to regression exactly when the shift re-weights whole strata (Corollary 1), and only approximately otherwise, which our experiments quantify.

# 3. Preliminaries and problem formulation

## 3.1 Stratum-wise coverage

Let $(X,Y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}$ have joint law $P$, with marginal law $P_X$ of the covariates. Table B1 in Appendix B lists the notation. A *prediction set* is a map $C$ from $\mathcal{X}$ to subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ whose graph $\{(x,y):y\in C(x)\}$ is jointly measurable. Split conformal prediction [@papadopoulos2002inductive; @lei2018distribution] guarantees *marginal* coverage $P\{Y\in C(X)\}\ge 1-\alpha$, and much recent work targets approximate *covariate-conditional* coverage $P\{Y\in C(X)\,|\, X=x\}\approx 1-\alpha$ [@romano2019conformalized; @izbicki2022cdsplit; @gibbs2025conformal], which cannot be guaranteed in finite samples without further assumptions [@barber2021limits].

In imbalanced regression the outcomes of interest are rare target values. Let $e_1<\dots<e_{K-1}$ be cut points and $B_1=(-\infty,e_1)$, $B_k=[e_{k-1},e_k)$ for $1<k<K$, $B_K=[e_{K-1},\infty)$ the resulting *target strata*, for instance the lower tail, the bulk and the upper tail, with $p_k=P(Y\in B_k)>0$. We call
$$\operatorname{Cov}_k(C)=P\{Y\in C(X)\,|\, Y\in B_k\},\qquad k=1,\ldots,K, \qquad (1)$$
the *stratum-wise* (target-conditional) coverage of $C$; we use the term stratum-wise throughout. It is the regression analogue of class-conditional coverage [@vovk2013conditional; @sadinle2019least] and the notion behind BCCP [@randahl2026bin] and equal opportunity of coverage [@wang2023equal]. Because $\sum_k p_k\,\operatorname{Cov}_k(C)=P\{Y\in C(X)\}$, marginal coverage only constrains a $p$-weighted average of the $\operatorname{Cov}_k$. In particular, a set with *exact* coverage $1-\alpha$, marginally or at every $x$, has $\min_k\operatorname{Cov}_k(C)\le 1-\alpha$, with equality only if all strata are covered at exactly the same rate; any over-coverage of the bulk is paid for in some other stratum.

## 3.2 Covariate-conditional validity does not imply stratum-wise validity

How large can the shortfall be for the best covariate-conditional set? The simplest model gives an answer.

**Proposition 1 (Gaussian model).** *Let $X\sim N(0,\tau^2)$ and $Y\,|\, X\sim N(X,\sigma^2)$ with $\tau^2+\sigma^2=1$ and $0<\tau^2<1$, so that $R^2=\tau^2$ is the fraction of variance explained by the optimal predictor. Let $\Phi$ be the standard normal distribution function, $z_\alpha=\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)$ and $C^{\mathrm{cov}}(x)=[x-z_\alpha\sigma,\,x+z_\alpha\sigma]$, which is, up to Lebesgue-null sets, the unique set of minimal length with $P\{Y\in C(x)\,|\, X=x\}\ge 1-\alpha$ for every $x$. Then*
$$P\{Y\in C^{\mathrm{cov}}(X)\,|\, Y=y\}=\Phi\!\left(\frac{z_\alpha\sigma-(1-R^2)y}{\tau\sigma}\right)-\Phi\!\left(\frac{-z_\alpha\sigma-(1-R^2)y}{\tau\sigma}\right), \qquad (2)$$
*an even function of $y$ that is strictly decreasing in $|y|$ and tends to $0$ as $|y|\to\infty$.*

The proof is in Appendix A.

Integrating (2) over strata of $Y$ (Table 2): for a predictor that explains half of the variance, the oracle covariate-conditional interval covers 70.5% of the top-10% outcomes and 37.5% of the top-1% outcomes, against 94.9% of the central 80%; with $R^2=0.9$ it covers 86.2% of the top decile. The shape of this profile is specific to the Gaussian model, so Table 2 also reports Monte Carlo values for heavy-tailed signals and skewed noise. With a Student-$t_3$ signal the top decile is still under-covered at $R^2=0.5$ (0.648), but the top 1% is covered at 0.625, because extreme outcomes then mostly come from extreme signals, which a covariate-conditional set follows. With right-skewed Gamma noise the oracle *over*-covers the lower decile (0.987) and covers only 0.418 of the top decile and 0.029 of the top 1%. In every case at least one tail stratum is covered well below $1-\alpha$. Tail under-coverage of standard conformal sets has been reported empirically [@wang2023equal; @randahl2026bin; @boruah2026distribution]; it persists for the oracle, so better covariate adaptivity cannot remove it. Coverage in rare strata requires calibration against the law of $(X,Y)$ *given* $Y\in B_k$, which is what stratified calibration does.

# 4. Proposed methods: stratified and relevance-weighted density-level conformal prediction

## 4.1 Stratified (Mondrian) calibration

Data are split into a proper training set $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ of size $N$, a calibration set $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}=\{(X_i,Y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ and a test set. On $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ we fit a nonconformity score $s:\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}$ (small values = conforming) and choose the cut points, e.g. at quantiles of the training targets. Let $k(y)$ be the stratum containing $y$, $I_k=\{i:Y_i\in B_k\}$, $n_k=|I_k|$, and, for levels $\alpha_k\in(0,1)$,
$$\hat q_k=\text{the }r_k\text{-th smallest value of }\{s(X_i,Y_i):i\in I_k\},\qquad r_k=\lceil (n_k+1)(1-\alpha_k)\rceil, \qquad (3)$$
with $\hat q_k=+\infty$ if $r_k>n_k$, which happens exactly when $n_k<1/\alpha_k-1$ (i.e. $n_k\le 8$ for $\alpha_k=0.1$). The stratified prediction set is
$$\hat C(x)=\{y\in\mathbb{R}: s(x,y)\le \hat q_{k(y)}\}=\bigcup_{k=1}^K\{y\in B_k: s(x,y)\le\hat q_k\}. \qquad (4)$$
The threshold applied to a candidate value $y$ is that of the stratum *of that candidate*, not of a prediction; this makes the guarantee stratum-wise. Equation (4) is an inductive Mondrian conformal predictor whose taxonomy assigns each example to the stratum of its label [@vovk2005alrw; @vovk2013conditional]; with the absolute-residual score it is BCCP [@randahl2026bin]. When every sub-level set $\{y:s(x,y)\le q\}$ is an interval, as for residual or quantile scores, $\hat C(x)$ is a union of at most $K$ intervals; density scores with a multimodal estimate can produce more pieces.

The following guarantee is the standard label-conditional Mondrian property [@vovk2013conditional], stated for outcome bins by @wang2023equal [Thm. 4.1] and @randahl2026bin. We restate it for an arbitrary score, with the edge cases used later (proof in Appendix A).

**Theorem 1 (stratum-wise validity of Mondrian calibration).** *Suppose that, conditionally on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$, the points $(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,(X_n,Y_n),(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})$ are exchangeable, and let $p_k=P(Y_{n+1}\in B_k\,|\,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}})$. Then for every $k$ with $p_k>0$,*
$$P\{Y_{n+1}\in\hat C(X_{n+1})\,|\, Y_{n+1}\in B_k,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}\}\ \ge\ 1-\alpha_k, \qquad (5)$$
*and consequently $P\{Y_{n+1}\in\hat C(X_{n+1})\,|\,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}\}\ge 1-\sum_kp_k\alpha_k\ge 1-\max_k\alpha_k$. If the scores within stratum $k$ are almost surely distinct and finite, then also $P\{Y_{n+1}\in\hat C(X_{n+1})\,|\, Y_{n+1}\in B_k,n_k,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}\}\le 1-\alpha_k+1/(n_k+1)$. The levels $\alpha_k$ may depend on $n_k$, and the score and strata may be any functions of $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$.*

The guarantee holds for *any* score. For target shift between calibration and deployment, known facts specialise as follows.

**Corollary 1 (target shift).** *Let $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$ be i.i.d. from $P$ and let the test point be drawn from $Q$, independently of $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$ and $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$. Write $P_k=P(\cdot\,|\, Y\in B_k)$ and $Q_k=Q(\cdot\,|\, Y\in B_k)$ for the within-stratum laws of the pair $(X,Y)$ and $\pi_k=Q(Y\in B_k)$; assume $p_k>0$ whenever $\pi_k>0$.*
*(i) If $Q_k=P_k$ for every $k$ with $\pi_k>0$ (the shift re-weights whole strata), then $Q\{Y\in\hat C(X)\,|\, Y\in B_k\}\ge 1-\alpha_k$ for every such $k$, and $Q\{Y\in\hat C(X)\}\ge 1-\sum_k\pi_k\alpha_k$ whatever the $\pi_k$.*
*(ii) For an arbitrary shift, $Q\{Y\in\hat C(X)\,|\, Y\in B_k\}\ge 1-\alpha_k-d_{\operatorname{TV}}(Q_k,P_k)$, where $d_{\operatorname{TV}}$ is the total-variation distance.*
*(iii) Under label shift, $Q(\cdot\,|\, Y=y)=P(\cdot\,|\, Y=y)$ with density ratio $w=dQ_Y/dP_Y$; let $m_k$ and $M_k$ be the essential infimum and supremum of $w$ on $B_k$, $0<m_k\le M_k<\infty$. Then $Q\{Y\notin\hat C(X)\,|\, Y\in B_k\}\le(M_k/m_k)\,\alpha_k$ and $d_{\operatorname{TV}}(Q_k,P_k)\le\tanh\{\log(M_k/m_k)/4\}$.*

The proof is in Appendix A. Part (i) is the continuous-outcome form of the label-shift robustness of label-conditional coverage [@podkopaev2021distribution; @angelopoulos2024theoretical]. For a continuous outcome, a label shift generally also changes the law of $Y$ *within* a stratum, so exactness requires an unchanged joint law of $(X,Y)$ inside each stratum. Part (ii) applies the coverage-gap bounds of @barber2023beyond [Sec. 4.5] within a stratum, and part (iii) follows from a bounded likelihood ratio. Both bounds are loose for the tilts of the target-shift experiment (Section 6.5), whose density ratios are unbounded on the open tail strata; they explain the direction of the effect, not its size. Marginally calibrated sets have no analogue of (i): their coverage under $Q$ is $\sum_k\pi_k\operatorname{Cov}_k$, which can be as low as $\min_k\operatorname{Cov}_k$. Stratified calibration needs no estimate of the shift, but it is exact only for shifts that act on the stratum probabilities.

## 4.2 The smallest sets with stratum-wise coverage

Theorem 1 leaves the score free; the score determines how large the sets are. For a prediction set $C$ let $|C(x)|$ be its Lebesgue measure and $\mathbb{E}|C(X)|=\int|C(x)|\,P_X(dx)$ its expected size. By the Neyman–Pearson argument behind conformal density level sets [@lei2013distribution], least-ambiguous classifiers [@sadinle2019least] and covariate-indexed length-optimal sets [@kiyani2024length], size-optimal sets are density level sets. The constraints in (1) are indexed by the *label*, so the problem separates by stratum and the thresholds are constant within each stratum.

**Proposition 2 (size-optimal stratum-wise sets).** *Suppose the conditional law of $Y$ given $X$ has a Lebesgue density $f(y\,|\, x)$, that $f(Y\,|\, X)>0$ almost surely, and that for each $k$ the random variable $f(Y\,|\, X)$ has a continuous distribution conditionally on $Y\in B_k$. Fix $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_K\in(0,1)$. Then there are thresholds $t_k>0$ with $P\{f(Y\,|\, X)\ge t_k\,|\, Y\in B_k\}=1-\alpha_k$, and the set*
$$C^\star(x)=\bigcup_{k=1}^K\{y\in B_k: f(y\,|\, x)\ge t_k\} \qquad (6)$$
*satisfies $\operatorname{Cov}_k(C^\star)=1-\alpha_k$ for all $k$. Every prediction set $C$ with $\operatorname{Cov}_k(C)\ge 1-\alpha_k$ for all $k$ satisfies $\mathbb{E}|C(X)\cap B_k|\ge\mathbb{E}|C^\star(X)\cap B_k|$ for every $k$, and hence $\mathbb{E}|C(X)|\ge\mathbb{E}|C^\star(X)|$.*

The proof (Appendix A) is the per-stratum Neyman–Pearson argument; it also covers data-dependent (e.g. conformal) sets whose stratum-wise coverage, averaged over the data, is at least $1-\alpha_k$. With $K=1$ it reduces to a single-threshold density level set, whereas HPD-split [@izbicki2022cdsplit] uses an $x$-specific threshold. Proposition 2 is an oracle statement. Stratified calibration of a uniformly consistent density estimate attains it asymptotically with fixed strata (Proposition 3, stated and proved in Appendix A, following the plug-in arguments of @lei2013distribution and @izbicki2022cdsplit).

Four consequences guide the method.

*The optimal score* is the conditional density, $s(x,y)=-f(y\,|\, x)$, with stratum-specific thresholds; a single threshold is optimal only for marginal coverage.

*The price of stratum-wise validity.* Every set that meets the stratum constraints has marginal coverage at least $1-\bar\alpha$, $\bar\alpha=\sum_kp_k\alpha_k$, so $\mathbb{E}|C^\star(X)|$ is at least the size of the optimal marginal set at that level, a single-threshold density level set. The two coincide if and only if $t_1=\dots=t_K$. With a common level $\alpha_k=\alpha$ the difference is the price, in size, of stratum-wise validity.

*Coverage is reallocated across inputs.* In a location–scale model $f(y\,|\, x)=g\{(y-\mu(x))/\sigma(x)\}/\sigma(x)$ the optimal set is, in standardised units $u=(y-\mu(x))/\sigma(x)$, $\{u: g(u)\ge t_k\sigma(x)\}$ within stratum $k$. Its standardised width *shrinks* as $\sigma(x)$ grows, and it is empty when $t_k\sigma(x)>\max g$: the oracle spends coverage where it is cheap. The stratified normalised residual (S-NCP), whose standardised width does not depend on $x$, is optimal only for constant $\sigma$. The density score's size advantage over S-NCP thus comes partly from the factor $1/\sigma(x)$ and is paid for by lower coverage, and occasionally empty sets, for the noisiest inputs. S-NCP spreads coverage more evenly over the noisiest inputs and is preferable when coverage of high-noise inputs matters.

*Shape and scope.* Because thresholds differ across strata, $C^\star(x)$ need not be an interval even for unimodal $f(\cdot\,|\, x)$. Optimality refers to size under the calibration covariate law; under a shifted $Q$, $C^\star$ stays valid in the sense of Corollary 1(i) but is no longer size-optimal. For targets with atoms (ties, zero inflation) Theorem 1 is unaffected, but a smooth density score is then valid without being size-optimal.

## 4.3 The SDLC procedure

Fig. 1 illustrates a prediction set and summarises the workflow of both methods.

![**Fig. 1.** (a) Prediction set of SDLC for one input $x$ (illustration): the estimated density $\hat f(y\,|\, x)$ is cut at a different height $-\hat q_k$ in each target stratum $B_k$, which can give a set of several pieces. (b) Workflow: the density model and the strata are fitted on the proper training set; calibration scores are grouped by the stratum of the observed target (SDLC, Eqs. 3–4) or weighted by the relevance $\phi(y)$ of the observed target (RW-DLC, Eq. 10); the test set is used only for evaluation. SDLC guarantees coverage within every stratum (Theorem 1) and RW-DLC relevance-weighted coverage (Theorem 2), both in finite samples.](/home/user/p_c1_2/Target_Journal_Submission/figures/F0_method_schematic.png){width=100%}


SDLC instantiates (3)–(4) with the plug-in score $s(x,y)=-\hat f(y\,|\, x)$, where $\hat f$ is estimated on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$; (5) holds for any $\hat f$. We use the semiparametric location–scale estimator
$$\hat f(y\,|\, x)=\frac{1}{\hat\sigma(x)}\,\hat g\!\left(\frac{y-\hat\mu(x)}{\hat\sigma(x)}\right). \qquad (7)$$
Here $\hat\mu$ is a gradient-boosted regression of $Y$ on $X$, and $\hat\sigma(x)=\max\{\tilde\sigma(x),\varsigma\}$, where $\tilde\sigma$ is a gradient-boosted regression of the absolute *out-of-fold* residuals $|Y-\hat\mu_{-j}(X)|$ (three folds), the spread estimate of locally weighted conformal prediction [@lei2018distribution]. The floor $\varsigma=\max\{10^{-3}\operatorname{sd}(Y_{\mathrm{tr}}),\,0.05\,\mathrm{median}|Y-\hat\mu_{-j}(X)|\}$ is shared by every method that uses $\hat\sigma$. The noise density is a defensive mixture
$$\hat g=(1-\lambda)\,\hat g_{\mathrm{KDE}}+\lambda\,\mathrm{St}_2(\cdot/\hat s_{\mathrm{IQR}})/\hat s_{\mathrm{IQR}},\qquad \lambda=0.01, \qquad (8)$$
of a Gaussian kernel density estimate (Silverman bandwidth) and the Student density with two degrees of freedom, $\mathrm{St}_2$, with robust scale $\hat s_{\mathrm{IQR}}=\mathrm{IQR}/1.349$. The mixture is fitted to *cross-fitted* standardised residuals: the out-of-fold residuals of fold $j$ are divided by a scale model trained on the other two folds, so that neither $\hat\mu$ nor $\hat\sigma$ has seen the residual it standardises (an in-sample scale fit shrinks the residuals, in our data by a median factor of 1.14 in standard deviation). This choice affects efficiency only, not validity, and was not tuned. The defensive component gives $\hat g$ polynomial tails, so that far-out targets still receive a smooth, strictly decreasing density. The nonparametric $\hat g$ captures skewness and heavy tails of the errors, and heteroscedasticity enters through $\hat\sigma$. If $Y=\mu(X)+\sigma(X)\varepsilon$ with noise $\varepsilon$ independent of $X$, and $\lambda$ and $\varsigma$ shrink to zero as $N$ grows, (7) can be uniformly consistent and Proposition 3 applies. Our implementation keeps $\lambda=0.01$ and the floor fixed and truncates to the domain $\mathcal{Y}_D$ (Section 4.4), so Proposition 3 describes the limit of a sequence of such estimators, not the fixed-constant implementation itself. Otherwise SDLC targets the optimal sets of the best location–scale approximation, which affects size but never validity.

Three ablations replace $\hat g$ by a standard normal density (SDLC-N, which isolates the factor $1/\hat\sigma(x)$ only approximately), by a two-piece density with side-specific scales (SDLC-2p), or by a histogram density from a 40-class gradient-boosted classifier (SDLC-hist) [cf. @sesia2021conformal; @guha2024conformal]; their single-threshold counterparts are DLS-N, DLS-2p and DLS-hist (Supplementary Table S9).

## 4.4 Implementation

*Strata.* The primary stratification uses the training-target quantiles $0.10, 0.25, 0.75, 0.90$ ($K=5$: lower tail, lower shoulder, bulk, upper shoulder, upper tail). With tied targets, coinciding cut points are merged, so realised strata can differ from the nominal ones; the sensitivity analyses repeat the main comparisons on datasets whose realised tail strata are close to nominal.

*Exact scores and the domain.* Calibration scores are computed exactly at the observed target, $S_i=-\hat f(Y_i\,|\, X_i)$. Sets are restricted to a bounded domain $\mathcal{Y}_D=[\min y-0.05\Delta_y,\max y+0.05\Delta_y]$ of the training targets ($\Delta_y$ the training range), intersected with $[0,\infty)$ when all training targets are non-negative. A point with $Y\notin\mathcal{Y}_D$ receives the score $+\infty$, stays in its stratum, and counts as not covered. Membership of a test target is decided by the same exact rule, $Y_{n+1}\in\hat C(X_{n+1})$ iff $s(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})\le\hat q_{k(Y_{n+1})}$, so the lower bound of Theorem 1 applies to the reported coverage, except that a test target outside $\mathcal{Y}_D$ always counts as a miss. When $\hat q_k=+\infty$ the reported set is $B_k\cap\mathcal{Y}_D$. We report the fraction of test targets outside $\mathcal{Y}_D$ and how often $\hat q_k=+\infty$ occurs.

*Set geometry.* Only the size, number of pieces, hull and tail alerts of a set are computed numerically. For a test point, $\hat C(x)\cap B_k=\{\hat\mu(x)+\hat\sigma(x)u:\hat g(u)\ge-\hat q_k\hat\sigma(x)\}\cap B_k$. We tabulate $\hat g$ on a non-uniform standardised grid with analytic Student tails, interpolate linearly, and split every cell exactly at the stratum boundaries and the bounds of $\mathcal{Y}_D$. A four times finer grid changes mean set sizes by at most 0.2% and individual set sizes by at most 1.5% (Supplementary Section S3 and Supplementary Table S4). The same construction is used for every location–scale baseline (DLS, DCP, HPD-split, S-HPD, SDLC-N).

**Algorithm 1.** Stratified density-level conformal prediction (SDLC).

> *Input:* proper training set $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$; calibration set $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$; stratum levels $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_K$; stratum probabilities (e.g. $0.10,0.25,0.75,0.90$).
> *Output:* prediction set $\hat C(x)$ for any test input $x$.
>
> 1. Fit the mean model $\hat\mu$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and compute three-fold out-of-fold residuals $Y-\hat\mu_{-j}(X)$.
> 2. Fit the scale model $\tilde\sigma$ on all absolute out-of-fold residuals and three cross-fitted scale models; set $\hat\sigma=\max\{\tilde\sigma,\varsigma\}$.
> 3. Standardise the out-of-fold residuals with the cross-fitted scales and fit the noise density $\hat g$ by Eq. (8); form $\hat f(y\,|\, x)$ by Eq. (7).
> 4. Compute the cut points from the training targets and define the strata $B_1,\dots,B_K$ and the domain $\mathcal{Y}_D$.
> 5. **for** each $(X_i,Y_i)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$ **do** $S_i\leftarrow-\hat f(Y_i\,|\, X_i)$ if $Y_i\in\mathcal{Y}_D$, else $S_i\leftarrow+\infty$; assign $i$ to stratum $k(Y_i)$.
> 6. **for** $k=1,\dots,K$ **do** $\hat q_k\leftarrow$ the $\lceil (n_k+1)(1-\alpha_k)\rceil$-th smallest score in stratum $k$ (Eq. 3; $+\infty$ if this rank exceeds $n_k$).
> 7. **return** $\hat C(x)=\bigcup_{k}\{y\in B_k\cap\mathcal{Y}_D:\hat f(y\,|\, x)\ge-\hat q_k\}$, computed stratum-exactly on the standardised grid.

## 4.5 Computational complexity

Fitting (steps 1–3) requires eight gradient-boosted regressions: one mean model, three out-of-fold mean models, one scale model and three cross-fitted scale models. The kernel density estimate costs $O(N+G_g\log G_g)$ by linear binning and FFT convolution on $G_g$ grid points. Calibration (steps 5–6) costs $O(n\log n)$. Prediction (step 7) inverts the density level on each of the $M$ monotone runs of the piecewise-linear $\hat g$ (typically a handful) within each of the $K$ strata, at $O(KM\log G_g)$ per test point, against $O(K)$ for stratified interval methods such as S-NCP. One fit serves every $\alpha$: changing $\alpha$ or the strata requires only recalibration.

RW-DLC (Section 4.7) has no comparable exact geometry, because its threshold $q(y)$ in (10) depends on the candidate through $\phi(y)$. Its coverage is decided exactly at the observed target, but its sets are computed on a uniform grid of $G$ target values over $\mathcal{Y}_D$ ($G=4{,}000$ in the experiments): $O(G\log G_g)$ per test point to interpolate $\hat g$ and $O(G)$ comparisons, with memory $O(cG)$ for a chunk of $c$ test points processed together. The relevance function adds set-up cost. With the exact Gaussian kernel density estimate used in our implementation, the floor $p_{\min}$ costs $O(N^2)$, the calibration weights $O(nN)$ and $\phi$ on the grid $O(GN)$; a binned estimate, as used for $\hat g$, would reduce this to $O(N+G\log G)$. The threshold $q$ on the grid is recomputed for each $\alpha$ or $\phi$ at $O(n\log n+G\log n)$, without refitting any model.

*Deployment.* SDLC and RW-DLC store eight gradient-boosted models, against five for S-NCP with an out-of-fold scale model, plus the tabulated $\hat g$ and the thresholds (RW-DLC also the weighted calibration scores and $q$ on the grid). Changing $\alpha$, the strata or $\phi$ requires recalibration only; a change of the feature distribution requires refitting. Prediction is independent across test points and parallelises trivially. Wall-clock times are reported in Section 6.7.

## 4.6 Interval (hull) and floor variants

*Hull variant (SDLC-hull).* When an interval is required, the convex hull of $\hat C(x)$ is reported. It inherits (5), but not the upper bound of Theorem 1 or the optimality of Proposition 2.

*Floor variant (SDLC-floor).* A user may want the efficiency of a single marginal threshold while protecting rare strata at a lower level $1-\beta$. The set $\hat C_{\mathrm{floor}}(x)=\{y: s(x,y)\le\max(\hat q^{\mathrm{marg}}_\alpha,\hat q_{k(y)}(\beta))\}$, with $\hat q^{\mathrm{marg}}_\alpha$ the ordinary split-conformal quantile at level $\alpha$ and $\hat q_k(\beta)$ the stratified quantile (3) at level $\beta$, contains both the marginal set and the stratified set at level $\beta$, so it has marginal coverage $\ge 1-\alpha$ and stratum-wise coverage $\ge 1-\beta$ in finite samples; we use $\beta=2\alpha$. This simple, conservative rule is not a contribution; level-optimising schemes [@wang2023equal; @kiyani2024length] are principled alternatives.

## 4.7 Relevance-weighted coverage without strata: RW-DLC

Stratified calibration protects each stratum, but its protection ends at the resolution of the strata: outcomes in the top 1% lie inside the upper stratum $B_K$ and are covered only on average with the rest of that stratum, and refining the strata quickly leaves too few calibration points per stratum. The imbalanced-regression literature describes the importance of outcomes not by strata but by a *relevance function* $\phi:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)$, for example the inverse of the target density [@torgo2007utility; @steininger2021denseweight]. We therefore also consider *relevance-weighted coverage*
$$\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(C)=\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\phi(Y)\,\mathbf{1}\{Y\in C(X)\}\big]}{\mathbb{E}[\phi(Y)]}, \qquad (9)$$
which, for $\phi=1/p_Y$, is coverage averaged uniformly over the range of the target, the continuous analogue of the macro-coverage that @bhattacharyya2026macro study for long-tailed classification. A stratum-wise guarantee for every stratum implies (9) for every $\phi$ that is constant on strata; (9) instead gives one averaged guarantee for a continuous relevance, without choosing strata.

Let $\phi$ be fixed given $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$, let $S_i=s(X_i,Y_i)$ be the calibration scores with weights $\phi_i=\phi(Y_i)$ and $W=\sum_i\phi_i$, and define for a candidate $y$
$$q(y)=\inf\Big\{\theta:\ \sum_{i=1}^n\phi_i\,\mathbf{1}\{S_i\le \theta\}\ \ge\ (1-\alpha)\big(W+\phi(y)\big)\Big\},\qquad \hat C_\phi(x)=\{y:\ s(x,y)\le q(y)\}, \qquad (10)$$
with $q(y)=+\infty$ when no such $\theta$ exists. The candidate's own weight sits at $+\infty$, as in weighted conformal prediction under covariate shift [@tibshirani2019conformal] and label shift [@podkopaev2021distribution].

**Theorem 2 (relevance-weighted validity).** *If $(X_1,Y_1),\ldots,(X_n,Y_n),(X,Y)$ are i.i.d. given $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and $0<\mathbb{E}\phi(Y)<\infty$, then $\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(\hat C_\phi)\big]\ge 1-\alpha$, the expectation being over $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$. Equivalently, $\hat C_\phi$ has coverage at least $1-\alpha$ for a test point drawn from the target-shifted law $Q$ with $dQ/dP=\phi(y)/\mathbb{E}\phi(Y)$.*

The proof (Appendix A) applies the weighted-exchangeability argument of @tibshirani2019conformal with the likelihood ratio $\phi(y)/\mathbb{E}\phi(Y)$, which depends only on the target. Two consequences matter in practice. First, (10) gives $q(y)=+\infty$ exactly when $\phi(y)>\alpha W/(1-\alpha)$: a candidate whose relevance exceeds the fraction $\alpha/(1-\alpha)$ of the total calibration relevance is always included. Rare targets are protected without strata, but only to the extent that the calibration set contains comparable relevance mass, which the Kish effective sample size $W^2/\sum_i\phi_i^2$ summarises. Second, Theorem 2 is also an exact guarantee under a target shift whose density ratio is proportional to $\phi$.

**Proposition 4 (size-optimal relevance-weighted sets).** *Under the density assumption of Proposition 2, among all set-valued functions with $\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(C)\ge1-\alpha$, the expected Lebesgue size $\mathbb{E}|C(X)|$ is minimised by $C^\star_\phi(x)=\{y:\ \phi(y)f(y\,|\, x)\ge t\}$, with $t$ chosen so that the constraint holds with equality (assuming the distribution of $\phi(Y)f(Y\,|\, X)$ has no atom at $t$).*

The proof is the Neyman–Pearson argument of Proposition 2 with the measure $\phi(y)f(y\,|\, x)\,dy\,dP_X$ in place of the stratum measure; its discrete counterpart for class-weighted coverage is given by @bhattacharyya2026macro. The proposition yields the score of *relevance-weighted density-level conformal prediction* (RW-DLC), $s(x,y)=-\phi(y)\hat f(y\,|\, x)$, calibrated by (10) and using the same density estimate (7) as SDLC. Relevance thus enters twice: it tilts the score towards relevant outcomes, which shapes the sets, and it weights the calibration, which delivers the guarantee. In the experiments $\phi$ is fitted on the training targets only, $\phi_U(y)=1/\max\{\hat p_Y(y),p_{\min}\}$ ("uniform over the target range") or $\phi_H=\phi_U^{1/2}$, with $\hat p_Y$ a Gaussian kernel density estimate and the floor $p_{\min}=0.01\max_i\hat p_Y(Y_i)$ (Section 5). The suffixes -U and -H mark the relevance function: RW-DLC-U and RW-DLC-H are RW-DLC with $\phi_U$ and $\phi_H$, and the ablations RW-DLS-U and RW-NCP-U use $\phi_U$.


# 5. Experimental design

The study answers seven research questions (RQ1–RQ7), reported in this order in Section 6. The datasets, seeds, splits, miscoverage levels, strata, base learners, methods and analyses of the main benchmark were fixed in a version-controlled configuration before it was run (Section 5.7).

* **RQ1 (tail coverage).** Do marginal and covariate-adaptive methods under-cover rare target strata, and does stratified calibration restore stratum-wise coverage?
* **RQ2 (price).** How much larger are the sets under stratum-wise validity, compared with marginal methods at the same and at a stricter level?
* **RQ3 (score).** Does the density score of Proposition 2 give smaller stratified sets than other scores, and which component is responsible? We answer it with an ablation on real data, a synthetic study and a confirmatory replication.
* **RQ4 (relevance-weighted coverage).** Does RW-DLC attain relevance-weighted coverage and cover the most extreme outcomes better than SDLC?
* **RQ5 (shift).** How robust is stratum-wise coverage under target shift?
* **RQ6 (decisions).** Are prediction sets useful for flagging extreme outcomes, compared with dedicated conformal tail classifiers and conformal selection?
* **RQ7 (sensitivity).** How sensitive are the conclusions to the miscoverage level, strata, calibration-set size, density estimator, base learner, imbalance-aware training and grouped splits?

## 5.1 Datasets

The main benchmark uses the OpenML-CTR23 suite of 35 curated tabular regression datasets [@fischer2023openml]; using a complete external suite avoids selecting datasets that favour the proposed method. By a pre-specified rule, we excluded the four datasets whose target takes fewer than 20 distinct values, because Lebesgue set size does not measure informativeness for such ordinal targets. The rule is a threshold on the number of distinct values only; count targets (e.g. abalone rings) and heavily discretised targets were retained. Targets with heavy ties or atoms are retained, such as the burned area of forest fires, which is zero for 47.8% of the fires. The remaining 31 datasets (Supplementary Table S1) have 517 to 72,000 observations and 5 to 971 features after one-hot encoding; 15 have a target skewness above 1 in absolute value.

*Confirmatory datasets.* For the replication, we took the regression datasets of the tabular benchmark of @grinsztajn2022tree (OpenML suites 335 and 336), removed every dataset sharing source data with CTR23 and duplicate versions, and applied the same exclusion rule. This left 17 held-out datasets (Supplementary Table S2). As pre-specified, datasets with more than 50,000 rows were reduced to one fixed random subsample of 50,000 rows shared by all seeds; the CTR23 suite, whose largest dataset has 72,000 rows, was used as curated. Checks made after the analysis (Supplementary Tables S2, S3 and S17b) found that two held-out datasets (yprop_4_1, topo_2_1) contain the same observations and target, so the 17 datasets represent 16 independent targets; that six targets were log-transformed by the curators; that two targets are codes rather than quantities (visualizing_soil, seattlecrime6, the latter with 87% duplicate rows); and that the collection has at least 4,209 rows and no extremely skewed target ($|$skewness$|\le$ 6.7, against up to 59.0 in CTR23).

## 5.2 Splitting, leakage control and repetitions

Each of the 10 predefined seeds defines a random partition into proper training (50%), calibration (25%) and test (25%) sets; the replication uses five seeds. Every model, stratum boundary and density estimate uses the proper training set only. Several datasets contain repeated units, for example exact duplicate rows (9.1% of sarcos), repeated videos (video transcoding) and repeat sales (King County). The guarantees therefore refer to a new row from the same pool, not to a new unit (Supplementary Section S2 and Supplementary Table S3). Ten datasets were used during development or to diagnose the implementation (Section 5.7); the main comparisons are repeated without them.

## 5.3 Base learners

All methods share the same LightGBM models [@ke2017lightgbm] with one fixed configuration (learning rate 0.05, 31 leaves, up to 2000 rounds with early stopping on a 10% internal validation split): a mean model, quantile models (for CQR), the scale models of Algorithm 1, and a multiclass model over 40 target bins (histogram density). No method receives specific tuning. The sensitivity analyses (RQ7) add a two-layer perceptron with the same pipeline and LightGBM trained with DenseWeight sample weights, which up-weight rare targets [@steininger2021denseweight].

## 5.4 Compared methods

*Marginal or covariate-adaptive calibration.* Split conformal prediction with absolute residuals (SCP) [@papadopoulos2002inductive]; normalised conformal prediction (NCP) [@lei2018distribution]; CQR [@romano2019conformalized]; Mondrian conformal regression with five bins of the point prediction $\hat y=\hat\mu(x)$ (Mondrian-ŷ) [cf. @bostrom2020mondrian; @boruah2026distribution]; DCP [@chernozhukov2021distributional]; HPD-split [@izbicki2022cdsplit]; and density level sets with a single threshold (DLS) [cf. @lei2013distribution]. DCP, HPD-split and DLS use the same location–scale density as SDLC, so the comparison isolates the calibration rule.

*Stratified calibration.* BCCP [@randahl2026bin], the absolute-residual score with stratum-wise thresholds, in union and hull form (BCCP-hull); the normalised residual with stratum-wise thresholds (S-NCP), in union and hull form; stratified CQR (S-CQR); the HPD score with stratum-wise thresholds (S-HPD); and SDLC with its hull and floor ($\beta=2\alpha$) variants. S-CQR omits the level optimisation of @wang2023equal and is not an implementation of BFQR. S-NCP is the information-matched comparator of SDLC: both use the same $\hat\mu$ and $\hat\sigma$.

*Ablations.* The chain BCCP → S-NCP → SDLC-N → SDLC adds, in turn, the scale $\hat\sigma(x)$, the density weighting $1/\hat\sigma(x)$ and the estimated noise shape. The histogram and two-piece densities are secondary results.

*Tail-alert rules (RQ6).* A set raises an *upper-tail alert* when it intersects the upper stratum $B_K$; for every method with stratum-wise coverage $1-\alpha$ the alert recall is at least $1-\alpha$. We compare such alerts with a class-conditional conformal tail classifier that thresholds $\hat P(Y\in B_K\,|\, x)$ from the location–scale model (TC-LS) or the histogram model (TC-H) to guarantee recall $1-\alpha$; conformal selection with Benjamini–Hochberg control of the false discovery rate at $q_{\mathrm{FDR}}\in\{0.1,0.2\}$ (CS-BH-0.1, CS-BH-0.2) [@jin2023selection]; and the point-prediction rule (Point), "alert if $\hat\mu(x)$ is in $B_K$". All methods are evaluated from the same cached model outputs with the exact rules of Section 4.4.

## 5.5 Evaluation metrics

For each test set we report:

* marginal coverage and stratum-wise coverage (1) in each calibration stratum;
* coverage in strata *not* used for calibration (training-target deciles, the top and bottom 5% and 1%) and in deciles of $\hat\sigma(x)$, a diagnostic of covariate-conditional coverage;
* mean set size divided by the training-target standard deviation (*normalised size*, also written size/sd($y$)), the share of empty sets and the number of disjoint pieces;
* tail-alert recall, alert rate, precision, and the expected cost per unit when a missed extreme outcome costs $c$ times a false alert;
* coverage under shift, by importance weighting of the test set: tilts $\omega_\gamma(y)=\exp(\gamma\zeta_y)$ ($\gamma\in\{1,2\}$), with $\zeta_y=\Phi^{-1}\{\hat F_Y(y)\}$ the normal score of $y$ under the training distribution $\hat F_Y$, and a *positive control* that multiplies the upper stratum by 5 and the lower by 0.5 without changing the within-stratum laws, so that Corollary 1(i) applies exactly. We report the Kish effective sample size of the weights.

Stratum coverages are averaged over seeds before taking the minimum across strata ("worst-stratum coverage"). Because the expected minimum of noisy estimates is at most the minimum of their expectations (Jensen's inequality), worst-stratum coverage is biased downwards [cf. @boruah2026null], we also report a *null reference*: the worst-stratum coverage expected under exact validity given the realised stratum sizes (Monte Carlo over the Beta–binomial law of split-conformal coverage).

## 5.6 Statistical analysis

Following @demsar2006statistical, the unit of analysis is the dataset: metrics are averaged over seeds and methods are compared across datasets. Set sizes are compared on the log scale by the geometric-mean ratio with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval, the Hodges–Lehmann estimate, wins and losses, and the sign test. Paired comparisons use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Holm's correction within each family [@holm1979simple]. One-sided tests are used only for the hypothesis that stratified calibration improves worst-stratum and tail coverage over marginal methods; comparisons among stratified methods are two-sided. Equivalence of worst-stratum coverage is assessed by two one-sided Wilcoxon tests (TOST) with a margin of 0.01. Friedman mean ranks are descriptive only [@benavoli2016should].

## 5.7 Protocol, deviations and confirmatory replication

*What was pre-specified.* We call an element of the analysis *pre-specified* if it was committed to version control before the corresponding evaluation was run; none was registered with a third party. The configuration of the main benchmark, the primary method and directional expectations for the research questions of the main benchmark (all except RQ4) were pre-specified in this sense.

*What changed afterwards.* (1) A loader bug that parsed string-typed features as missing was corrected and the affected datasets recomputed. (2) After the first run we learned of BCCP and BFQR; BCCP's hull form, decile strata and tail-alert metrics were added. (3) The first implementation evaluated density-based sets on a coarse grid of the *marginal* target distribution, which inflated them; under it the size comparison with BCCP was not significant (Supplementary Section S5). (4) A review of the second run led to corrections applied to every method: cross-fitted standardisation, a defensive $t$ component instead of an ad hoc floor, half-open stratum pieces, a truncated domain and stratum-exact geometry. All reported results use the final implementation.

*Consequences.* Because the implementation changed after results had been seen, every size comparison on CTR23 (RQ3) is exploratory; the coverage results are unaffected in direction. To test the size comparisons, we committed a confirmatory analysis plan (hypotheses, tests, decision rules and script; Supplementary Section S6) to version control *before* evaluating any conformal method on the 17 held-out datasets; its commit identifier (`ccba99b`), commit time and the SHA-256 hash of the committed plan are listed in the anonymised code package (file PLAN_TIMESTAMPS.md); the version history itself will be made public upon acceptance. The time stamp is that of our repository, not of a third-party registry, so we call the plan pre-specified rather than pre-registered; base-model test metrics had already been computed on these datasets. Section 6.3.1 reports the outcome.

*Relevance-weighted study (RQ4).* RW-DLC was developed after the main benchmark had been run. Its analysis plan (relevance functions, comparators, metrics, hypotheses RW-1 to RW-4, tests and decision rules; Supplementary Section S7) was committed (identifier `a9f31d2`, listed in the same file) before RW-DLC was evaluated on any dataset, but after the SDLC results on the same held-out datasets, including its under-coverage of the top 1%, were known. RW-1 and RW-2 therefore test consequences of Theorem 2 and RW-3 an anticipated effect; only RW-4 could fail for empirical reasons, and it did (Section 6.3.1). RW-DLC, its ablations RW-DLS (score $-\hat f$) and RW-NCP (normalised residual), both with relevance-weighted calibration, and the comparators SCP, NCP, CQR, DLS (labelled DLS-M in the analysis plan), S-NCP and SDLC were evaluated with coverage decided exactly at the observed target and set sizes computed on a grid of 4,000 target values over $\mathcal{Y}_D$. Additional metrics are the relevance-weighted coverage (9), the mean and minimum coverage over training-target deciles, and coverage of the top and bottom 1%.

## 6.1 Marginal and covariate-adaptive methods under-cover the tails (RQ1)

All methods attained their nominal marginal coverage, but coverage of the target strata differed sharply. The seven marginal or covariate-adaptive methods covered 69.0%–82.0% of the targets in the upper stratum $B_K$ and 44.5%–65.5% of the top 1%, and their worst-stratum coverage was 63.0%–78.3%. On 26–31 of the 31 datasets, it was below the range expected under exact validity. Marginal methods over-cover the central deciles and pay for it in the outer ones (Fig. 2); covariate adaptivity reduces the shortfall but does not remove it. Every stratified method, in contrast, covered 90.6%–90.9% of the upper stratum, with worst-stratum coverage 89.0%–89.3%, and was below the null range on at most 2 datasets, as expected under exact validity. SDLC's worst-stratum coverage exceeded that of each marginal method on at least 29 of 31 datasets (one-sided Holm-adjusted $p<0.001$; Hodges–Lehmann differences 0.081–0.250). The result is unchanged without the development datasets ($p<0.001$) and for coverage of the upper stratum alone ($p<0.001$). SDLC covered 86.8% of the top 5% and 73.6% of the top 1%, against at most 77.0% and 65.5% for the marginal methods; the top 1% is not calibrated, and its coverage stays below the nominal level for every method.

## 6.4 Relevance-weighted coverage of the most extreme outcomes (RQ4)

Stratified calibration left the most extreme outcomes under-covered (top-1% coverage of SDLC 0.688 on the held-out datasets). RW-DLC was evaluated under its own pre-specified plan (Section 5.7); Table 8 reports the held-out datasets (17 datasets, 85 runs); Supplementary Table S18 lists the hypothesis tests.

* *RW-1 (validity).* The relevance-weighted coverage of RW-DLC was 0.898 on average, equivalent to the nominal 0.90 within the pre-specified margin of 0.02 (TOST $p$ < 0.001), as Theorem 2 predicts.
* *RW-2 (marginal methods miss the weighted target).* RW-DLC exceeded every marginal method in relevance-weighted coverage, by 0.122 (NCP) to 0.180 (DLS) on average (Holm-adjusted $p<0.001$ for all four comparisons).
* *RW-3 (extreme tail).* RW-DLC covered 0.872 of the top 1% of outcomes, against 0.688 for SDLC (mean difference 0.184, 95% CI 0.063–0.310, higher on 13 of 15 datasets with top-1% test points, one-sided $p$ = 0.011). The definition-sensitivity of RW-3 is reported in Supplementary Section S8.3 (it held with the main-pipeline definition of the top 1%).
* *RW-4 (score).* RW-DLC's sets were smaller than those of the two ablations that use relevance-weighted calibration with an unweighted score, RW-DLS (geometric-mean ratio 0.885) and RW-NCP (0.904), but after Holm correction neither difference reached the pre-specified threshold ($p$ = 0.053 for both), so RW-4 was not supported.

The guarantee has a price elsewhere. Because RW-DLC averages coverage over the target range, it spends less on the bulk: its marginal coverage was 0.884 (minimum over datasets 0.750) and its worst coverage over target deciles was lower than that of SDLC (mean difference -0.080). Its sets were not reliably larger than SDLC's (geometric-mean ratio 1.081, Hodges–Lehmann 1.113, median 1.174, 95% CI 0.916–1.240, descriptive). No test point had an infinite threshold; the smallest effective calibration sample size was 120 (median 1,368). The milder relevance $\phi_H$ gave intermediate results. RW-DLC and SDLC thus answer different requirements: SDLC guarantees every stratum, and RW-DLC guarantees an average over the whole target range that reaches the outcomes beyond the strata (Fig. 6).

*Dedicated tail strata (post hoc).* RW-DLC's advantage in RQ4 is over SDLC with the five primary strata, whose highest stratum is the top 10%. Stratified calibration can target the top 1% directly: with stratum edges at the training quantiles 0.01, 0.10, 0.25, 0.75, 0.90 and 0.99, the 1% strata received enough calibration points for a finite threshold on all but 1 held-out dataset, where the method returned the whole stratum in some seeds. Evaluated from the cached model outputs (Supplementary Table S17b), SDLC with these seven strata covered 0.906 of the top 1% (minimum over datasets 0.87), against 0.884 for RW-DLC, with a marginal coverage of 0.901 against 0.884; RW-DLC's sets were not reliably smaller (geometric-mean ratio 0.952, Hodges–Lehmann 0.987, smaller on 9 of 17 datasets). When the region of interest is a known extreme quantile range, dedicated strata deliver its coverage at least as well as relevance weighting. RW-DLC's distinct use is a guarantee averaged over the whole target range, without a choice of strata, and protection that degrades gradually rather than at stratum edges.

## 6.5 Target shift (RQ5)

Under the positive control, which re-weights whole strata, the stratum-wise coverage of every stratified method was unchanged, as Corollary 1(i) states, and their marginal coverage stayed at the nominal level (0.904 for SDLC), whereas that of the marginal methods fell to 0.838–0.877 (Fig. 7). ## 6.6 Tail alerts and decisions (RQ6)

Every stratified method guarantees an upper-tail alert recall of at least 0.9; their mean recall was 0.978–0.988, and it was at least 0.9 on all 31 datasets (Supplementary Table S23). The price is a high alert rate. Among the stratified methods SDLC raised fewer alerts than BCCP (25/5 datasets) and S-CQR, but more than S-NCP (12/18). The dedicated tail classifier TC-LS attains the guaranteed recall almost exactly (mean 0.906) and raised fewer alerts than SDLC on every dataset (alert-rate ratio 1.559); for a pure yes/no decision it is the more efficient tool. Conformal selection [@jin2023selection] controls the false discovery rate instead and flagged few units, with high precision (mean 0.766 at $q_{\mathrm{FDR}}=0.2$) but a mean recall of only 0.603; the point-prediction rule (Point) behaved similarly (recall 0.625).

The preferable rule depends on the cost $c$ of a miss relative to a false alert (Fig. 8; details in Supplementary Section S8.5). With equal costs, rules that raise few alerts (the point rule, conformal selection) were cheapest. At $c=10$ SDLC's expected cost did not differ significantly from that of TC-LS, and at $c=20$ SDLC was cheaper on most datasets, because its recall exceeds the guaranteed 0.9; among the stratified scores, however, S-NCP gave the cheapest alerts.

## 6.7 Sensitivity analyses (RQ7)

*Robustness.* The conclusions held at $\alpha=0.05$ and $0.2$, with three or ten strata, with a quarter of the calibration set (larger sets, because sparse tail strata then return the whole stratum), with a two-layer perceptron instead of gradient boosting, and with imbalance-aware DenseWeight training, which improved the tail coverage of marginal methods but did not remove the shortfall. Splits that keep repeated units together or hold out whole months changed little, but splitting video transcoding by video and Moneyball forward in time enlarged all sets several-fold and removed SDLC's size advantage (SDLC larger than BCCP). The price of stratification rose with target skewness and fell with calibration-set size; SDLC was larger than S-NCP only on small or heavily tied datasets. Details are in Supplementary Section S8.6–S8.7 and Tables S5–S8 and S15.
*Computational cost.* Computing a prediction set took a median of 13.5 µs per test point for SDLC, against 0.09 µs for S-NCP and 0.07 µs for BCCP (Supplementary Tables S13 and S14). SDLC is roughly two orders of magnitude slower than interval methods at prediction time, but the cost is negligible next to model fitting.


# 7. Discussion

## 7.1 Practical guidance

For practitioners who must report how often extreme outcomes are covered, we suggest the following.

1. *Decide which guarantee is required.* Marginal coverage, however adaptive the score, does not protect rare targets, and lowering $\alpha$ is a less efficient substitute. If every target range must be covered at the nominal level, calibrate within strata (SDLC or S-NCP). If a known extreme range such as the top 1% matters most and each such stratum receives at least $1/\alpha-1$ calibration points, give it its own stratum; this covered the top 1% at the nominal level on every held-out dataset. If coverage averaged over the whole target range is required without choosing strata, use RW-DLC with an inverse-density relevance, at the price of lower coverage in the bulk.
2. *Choose strata from training-target quantiles* so that each stratum receives several times $1/\alpha$ calibration points; otherwise use fewer strata or the floor variant.
3. *Use a score that includes the scale $\hat\sigma(x)$.* SDLC, S-NCP and S-HPD gave stratum-valid sets within a few percent of each other; SDLC was smallest on most datasets, while S-NCP is simpler, spreads coverage more evenly across noise levels, covers more of the extreme 1% and gives cheaper alerts. The residual score of BCCP and stratified CQR were clearly less efficient. When an interval is required, the hull of S-NCP was as small as the hull of SDLC on held-out data.
4. *Report hulls when an interval is mandatory.* Stratified sets can be discontiguous: SDLC's sets had 1.53 pieces on average (BCCP 1.47), mostly an isolated piece in a tail stratum whose threshold is lower than that of its neighbour (Supplementary Fig. S4). Such a piece says that an extreme outcome is plausible, but an interval is easier to communicate. SDLC-hull is valid and smaller than BCCP-hull, but loses part of the advantage.
5. *For yes/no decisions about extremes, match the rule to the costs.* A class-conditional conformal tail classifier attains the same recall guarantee with fewer alerts and is the better choice when false alerts are expensive; among set-based alerts, S-NCP's were the cheapest. Set-based alerts have higher recall, are cheaper when misses are very costly, and carry more information about the size of the outcome.

## 7.2 Limitations

*Size cost and data requirements.* Stratum-wise validity is paid for in set size, more so for skewed targets and small tail strata; with fewer than $1/\alpha_k-1$ calibration points the method returns the whole stratum. Pooling sparse strata [@ding2023class], cross-fitted calibration [@barber2021jackknife] and overlapping groups [@bairaktari2025kandinsky] are natural remedies that we did not evaluate.

*Granularity of the guarantee.* The guarantee holds for the calibrated strata, not for arbitrary sub-ranges. Within a stratum, coverage is lower near its outer end (Remark A.1 in Appendix A), and shifts that move mass within a stratum reduce coverage (Supplementary Table S22); Corollary 1 explains the direction of this effect, but its bounds are loose for open tail strata. The guarantee is also marginal over $X$ within each stratum, so it says nothing about a particular input, and the density score deliberately trades coverage across inputs.

*Density estimation and baselines.* Proposition 2 is an oracle property, and Proposition 3 requires a uniformly consistent density estimate, which the location–scale model provides only when the noise shape does not depend on $x$; flexible conditional density estimators may close the gap to the oracle. We did not stratify further covariate-adaptive methods such as conditional histograms [@sesia2021conformal] or localised conformal prediction [@hore2025local]; they cannot beat the oracle density score, but their estimated versions may be competitive.

*Evaluation scope.* The benchmark covers tabular targets with at least 20 distinct values; the rule excluded ordinal targets with few levels, but count, heavily discretised, coded and time-indexed targets were retained (Section 5.1). The held-out replication covered datasets with 4,209 to 50,000 rows, six with curator-supplied log-scale targets and none with the extreme skewness of some CTR23 targets, so it does not test the regimes in which the CTR23 size advantage disappeared (small or strongly skewed targets). All splits are random splits of one pool: the guarantees and the efficiency results hold for new rows from the same pool, not for new units or future periods. Five held-out datasets are hourly or daily series, and under grouped splits coverage varied widely between calibration sets (for video transcoding, SDLC's worst-stratum coverage ranged from 0.75 to 0.89 over seeds; Supplementary Table S15). All results use split calibration and one untuned base-learner configuration. The density-based implementation changed after earlier results had been seen (Section 5.7), which is why the CTR23 size comparisons are exploratory and were replicated on held-out data.

*Applicability of the guarantee.* Stratum-wise coverage requires exchangeability of calibration and test data, holds on average over calibration sets and over the test points of a stratum, and does not protect against covariate shift within strata.


# 8. Conclusion and future work

Prediction intervals that are valid on average can fail exactly where imbalanced regression needs them: on the rare outcomes. Even the oracle interval with exact covariate-conditional coverage under-covers the tails of the target, and on 31 benchmark datasets marginal and covariate-adaptive conformal methods covered only 63.0%–78.3% of the outcomes in their worst-covered stratum at a nominal 90%. We studied two guarantees that protect rare outcomes and the sets that meet them most efficiently.

Calibrating thresholds within strata of the target, an established technique known as Mondrian conformal prediction, guarantees coverage in every stratum for any score. For this guarantee, conditional-density level sets with stratum-specific thresholds are the smallest sets for the oracle, and SDLC, their plug-in version, gave smaller sets than bin-conditional conformal prediction with residual scores and than stratified quantile regression, on most CTR23 datasets and on 17 held-out datasets under a pre-specified analysis; the held-out collection contained neither small nor extremely skewed targets, and all splits were random splits of one pool. Among scores that use the estimated scale, however, the differences were within a few percent, and the simpler stratified normalised residual covered noisy inputs and the most extreme outcomes better. Strata also protect only at their own resolution. Relevance-weighted calibration avoids choosing strata: RW-DLC guarantees coverage averaged by an imbalanced-regression relevance function in finite samples, uses the score that is size-optimal for that guarantee, and in a second pre-specified study covered 88.4% of the top 1% of outcomes on held-out data, against 76.0% for SDLC with five strata, at a cost in coverage of the bulk. Dedicated 1% tail strata, however, covered the top 1% at least as well with sets of similar size, so the case for RW-DLC rests on its range-wide guarantee rather than on the extreme tail. For yes/no decisions about extremes, dedicated conformal tail classifiers attain the same recall guarantee with fewer alerts.

Future work should combine the two guarantees, for example a relevance-weighted guarantee with a floor on every stratum; learn relevance functions or strata that minimise size subject to validity; replace the location–scale density by flexible conditional density estimators, whose gap to the oracle was largest under covariate-dependent multimodality; and extend both guarantees to grouped and dependent data, where our grouped and temporal splits showed that efficiency depends on transfer to unseen units and periods.


# Appendix A. Proofs

**Proof of Proposition 1.** $(X,Y)$ is bivariate normal with $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=1$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\tau^2$, hence $X\,|\, Y=y\sim N(\tau^2y,\tau^2\sigma^2)$ and $Y-X\,|\, Y=y\sim N((1-\tau^2)y,\tau^2\sigma^2)$. Since $Y\in C^{\mathrm{cov}}(X)$ iff $|Y-X|\le z_\alpha\sigma$, (2) follows. Write $a=z_\alpha\sigma>0$, $b=\tau\sigma>0$ and $m=(1-R^2)y$. The function $h(m)=\Phi((a-m)/b)-\Phi((-a-m)/b)$ is even because $\Phi(-u)=1-\Phi(u)$, and for $m>0$, $h'(m)=-\{\Phi'((a-m)/b)-\Phi'((a+m)/b)\}/b<0$, because $|a-m|<a+m$ and the standard normal density $\Phi'$ decreases strictly in $|u|$. As $1-R^2>0$, (2) is strictly decreasing in $|y|$ and vanishes as $|y|\to\infty$. Minimality and uniqueness of $C^{\mathrm{cov}}(x)$ follow because the $N(x,\sigma^2)$ density is symmetric and strictly unimodal. $\square$

**Proof of Theorem 1.** Condition on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$, which fixes the score $s$ and the strata. Let $E_k=\{Y_{n+1}\in B_k\}$ and $J=\{i\le n+1: Y_i\in B_k\}$, so that $|J|=n_k+1$ on $E_k$. Membership of a point in $B_k$ is a function of that point alone, so any permutation of $\{1,\ldots,n+1\}$ that maps $J$ onto itself leaves the event $\{J=j,\ n+1\in J\}$ unchanged; by exchangeability, conditionally on $J=j\ni n+1$ and on the multiset $\{(X_i,Y_i):i\in j\}$, the index $n+1$ is uniformly distributed over $j$. Let $\mathcal{R}$ be the rank of $S_{n+1}=s(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})$ among $\{S_i\}_{i\in J}$, with ties broken uniformly at random; conditionally as above, $\mathcal{R}$ is uniform on $\{1,\ldots,n_k+1\}$. By (3)–(4), $Y_{n+1}\in\hat C(X_{n+1})$ iff $S_{n+1}\le\hat q_k$, which holds whenever $\mathcal{R}\le r_k=\lceil(n_k+1)(1-\alpha_k)\rceil$ (if $r_k>n_k$, then $\hat q_k=+\infty$ and coverage is one). Hence the conditional coverage is at least $r_k/(n_k+1)\ge 1-\alpha_k$. Averaging over $J$ and the multiset gives (5); since the argument conditions on $J$, the level $\alpha_k$ may depend on $n_k$. If scores are almost surely distinct, coverage is exactly $\min\{r_k,n_k+1\}/(n_k+1)<1-\alpha_k+1/(n_k+1)$ given $n_k$. The marginal statement follows from the law of total probability, $\sum_kp_k(1-\alpha_k)=1-\sum_kp_k\alpha_k$. Finally, the conditioning on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ allows any score and strata that are measurable functions of $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ alone. $\square$

**Proof of Corollary 1.** Given $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$ the set $A=\{(x,y):y\in\hat C(x)\}$ is fixed. Because the test point is independent of the calibration data, $Q\{Y\in\hat C(X)\,|\, Y\in B_k\}=\mathbb{E}\,Q_k(A)$ and the left-hand side of (5) equals $\mathbb{E}\,P_k(A)$, where $\mathbb{E}$ averages over $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}\sim P^n$ (i.i.d. data are exchangeable, so (5) applies). (i) If $Q_k=P_k$ the two expectations coincide, and the law of total probability gives the marginal statement. (ii) $|Q_k(A)-P_k(A)|\le d_{\operatorname{TV}}(Q_k,P_k)$ for every fixed $A$; average and use (5). (iii) Under label shift the likelihood ratio of $Q_k$ to $P_k$ is $\ell_k(y)=w(y)/\bar w_k$ with $\bar w_k=\pi_k/p_k=\mathbb{E}_{P_k}w(Y)\ge m_k$. Hence $Q_k(A^c)=\mathbb{E}_{P_k}\{\ell_k(Y)\mathbf{1}_{A^c}(X,Y)\}\le(M_k/\bar w_k)P_k(A^c)\le(M_k/m_k)P_k(A^c)$; averaging over $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$ and using (5) gives the multiplicative bound. For the total-variation bound, $\ell_k\in[a,b]$ with $a=m_k/\bar w_k\le1\le b=M_k/\bar w_k$ and $\mathbb{E}_{P_k}\ell_k=1$; since $d_{\operatorname{TV}}(Q_k,P_k)=\mathbb{E}_{P_k}(\ell_k-1)_+$ and, by convexity, $(\ell-1)_+\le(b-1)(\ell-a)/(b-a)$ on $[a,b]$, we obtain $d_{\operatorname{TV}}\le(b-1)(1-a)/(b-a)=(M_k-\bar w_k)(\bar w_k-m_k)/\{\bar w_k(M_k-m_k)\}$. The right-hand side is maximised at $\bar w_k=\sqrt{m_kM_k}$, where it equals $(\sqrt{M_k}-\sqrt{m_k})/(\sqrt{M_k}+\sqrt{m_k})=\tanh\{\log(M_k/m_k)/4\}$. $\square$

**Proof of Proposition 2.** Existence: $G_k(t)=P\{f(Y\,|\, X)\ge t\,|\, Y\in B_k\}$ is continuous and non-increasing with $G_k(0^+)=1$ and $G_k(t)\to0$ as $t\to\infty$, so the intermediate value theorem gives $t_k>0$ with $G_k(t_k)=1-\alpha_k$. Optimality: write $A=\{(x,y):y\in C(x)\}$, $A^\star$ likewise, $A_k=A\cap(\mathcal{X}\times B_k)$, $A_k^\star=A^\star\cap(\mathcal{X}\times B_k)$ and $|A|_P=\iint\mathbf{1}_A(x,y)\,dy\,P_X(dx)$, so that $\mathbb{E}|C(X)\cap B_k|=|A_k|_P$ and $p_k\operatorname{Cov}_k(C)=\iint\mathbf{1}_{A_k}f(y\,|\, x)\,dy\,P_X(dx)$. Since $|\{y:f(y\,|\, x)\ge t_k\}|\le 1/t_k$, $|A_k^\star|_P<\infty$; if $|A_k|_P=\infty$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise the integrand of
$$\Delta_k=\iint\left(\mathbf{1}_{A_k^\star}-\mathbf{1}_{A_k}\right)\left(f(y\,|\, x)-t_k\right)dy\,P_X(dx)$$
is non-negative pointwise: on $A_k^\star\setminus A_k$ we have $f\ge t_k$, and on $A_k\setminus A_k^\star$, where $y\in B_k$, we have $f<t_k$. Hence $\Delta_k\ge 0$, i.e. $t_k(|A_k^\star|_P-|A_k|_P)\le p_k\{\operatorname{Cov}_k(C^\star)-\operatorname{Cov}_k(C)\}\le 0$, and $t_k>0$ gives $|A_k^\star|_P\le|A_k|_P$. Summing over $k$ gives the statement for $\mathbb{E}|C(X)|$. $\square$

**Proposition 3 (asymptotic size-optimality of the plug-in procedure).** *Let the assumptions of Proposition 2 hold, and suppose in addition that the distribution function of $f(Y\,|\, X)$ given $Y\in B_k$ is strictly increasing at $t_k$ for every $k$. Let $\hat f$ be estimated from $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ of size $N$, let $\xi_N=\sup_{x,y}|\hat f(y\,|\, x)-f(y\,|\, x)|\to0$ in probability, and let $\hat C$ be the stratified set (3)–(4) with score $s=-\hat f$, fixed strata $B_1,\ldots,B_K$ and $n\to\infty$ calibration points. Then $-\hat q_k\to t_k$ in probability for every $k$, and*
$$\mathbb{E}_X\big|\hat C(X)\,\triangle\,C^\star(X)\big|\ \to\ 0\quad\text{in probability,} \tag{A.1}$$
*where $\mathbb{E}_X$ averages over a new $X\sim P_X$ given the data. In particular $\mathbb{E}_X|\hat C(X)|\to\mathbb{E}|C^\star(X)|$ while (5) holds for every $n$.*

**Proof of Proposition 3.** Condition on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ (hence on $\hat f$) and write $V_i=f(Y_i\,|\, X_i)$, $\hat V_i=\hat f(Y_i\,|\, X_i)$, so that $|\hat V_i-V_i|\le\xi_N$. *Thresholds.* By (3), $-\hat q_k$ is the $(n_k+1-r_k)$-th smallest of $\{\hat V_i:i\in I_k\}$, and $(n_k+1-r_k)/n_k\to\alpha_k$ because $n_k\to\infty$ almost surely ($n_k\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p_k)$ given $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$). Order statistics are 1-Lipschitz in the sup-norm, so $-\hat q_k$ differs by at most $\xi_N$ from the same order statistic of $\{V_i:i\in I_k\}$. The $V_i$, $i\in I_k$, are i.i.d. from the law of $f(Y\,|\, X)$ given $Y\in B_k$, independent of $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$, whose $\alpha_k$-quantile $t_k$ is unique by assumption; hence that order statistic converges to $t_k$ almost surely [@serfling1980approximation, Sec. 2.3], and $\delta_k:=|\hat q_k+t_k|\to0$ in probability. *Sets.* Fix $x$ and $y\in B_k$. If $y$ lies in exactly one of $\hat C(x)$ and $C^\star(x)$, then $\hat f(y\,|\, x)+\hat q_k$ and $f(y\,|\, x)-t_k$ have opposite signs (one is $\ge0$, the other $<0$), while they differ by at most $\xi_N+\delta_k$; hence $|f(y\,|\, x)-t_k|\le\eta:=\xi_N+\max_k\delta_k$. Therefore
$$\mathbb{E}_X|\hat C(X)\triangle C^\star(X)|\le\sum_{k=1}^K h_k(\eta),\qquad h_k(\eta)=\mathbb{E}\,\big|\{y\in B_k:|f(y\,|\, X)-t_k|\le\eta\}\big|.$$
For $\eta<t_k/2$ the set inside $h_k$ lies in $\{y:f(y\,|\, X)\ge t_k/2\}$, whose measure is at most $2/t_k$, and it decreases to $\{y\in B_k:f(y\,|\, X)=t_k\}$ as $\eta\downarrow0$. By dominated convergence $h_k(\eta)\to\mathbb{E}|\{y\in B_k:f(y\,|\, X)=t_k\}|$, which is zero: continuity of the law of $f(Y\,|\, X)$ given $Y\in B_k$ gives $0=p_k^{-1}\iint_{B_k}\mathbf{1}\{f(y\,|\, x)=t_k\}f(y\,|\, x)\,dy\,P_X(dx)=p_k^{-1}t_k\,\mathbb{E}|\{y\in B_k:f(y\,|\, X)=t_k\}|$. Since $\eta\to0$ in probability, (A.1) follows, and $|\,|\hat C|-|C^\star|\,|\le|\hat C\triangle C^\star|$ gives the statement on expected size. $\square$

**Remark A.1 (within-stratum coverage profile).** Under label shift, let $\kappa(y)=P\{y\in\hat C(X_{n+1})\,|\, Y_{n+1}=y\}$, which is the same under $P$ and $Q$. Then the change in stratum-wise coverage equals the within-stratum covariance $\mathbb{E}_{P_k}[\{\kappa(Y)-c_0\}\{\ell_k(Y)-1\}]$ for any constant $c_0$, with $\ell_k$ the likelihood ratio of the proof of Corollary 1, so coverage is lost only if the shift moves mass within $B_k$ towards values that are covered less often than the stratum average. For the oracle sets $C^\star$ in the Gaussian model of Proposition 1, $C^\star(x)\cap B_k=\{y\in B_k:|y-x|\le a_k\}$ for a stratum-specific radius $a_k$, so the argument of Proposition 1 (with $a=a_k$) shows that $\kappa$ decreases in $|y|$ within every stratum, that is, towards the outer end of a tail stratum; for the estimated sets the same pattern is observed empirically (Section 6.5). This is why a tilt towards more extreme outcomes can lower tail-stratum coverage even after stratified calibration.

**Proof of Theorem 2.** Write $Z_i=(X_i,Y_i)$ and let $Z_{n+1}=(X,Y)$ be drawn from $Q$ with $dQ/dP(z)=w(z)=\phi(y)/\mathbb{E}\phi(Y)$, independently of $Z_1,\ldots,Z_n\sim P$; conditionally on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}$ the score $s$ and $\phi$ are fixed. The joint law of $(Z_1,\ldots,Z_{n+1})$ has density $\prod_{i\le n}p(z_i)\cdot p(z_{n+1})w(z_{n+1})$ with respect to the product of the dominating measures, so the vector is weighted exchangeable with weight functions $(1,\ldots,1,w)$ [@tibshirani2019conformal, Lemma 3]. Hence, conditionally on the multiset $\mathcal{E}=\{z_1,\ldots,z_{n+1}\}$ of observed values, $P(Z_{n+1}=z_j\,|\, \mathcal{E})=w(z_j)/\sum_lw(z_l)=\phi(y_j)/\sum_l\phi(y_l)$, and the test score $S_{n+1}$ is distributed as $\sum_j\tilde\pi_j\delta_{S_j}$ with $\tilde\pi_j=\phi(y_j)/\sum_l\phi(y_l)$. Therefore $P\{S_{n+1}\le \mathrm{Quant}_{1-\alpha}(\sum_{j\le n+1}\tilde\pi_j\delta_{S_j})\,|\, \mathcal{E}\}\ge1-\alpha$, where $\mathrm{Quant}_{1-\alpha}$ denotes the $(1-\alpha)$-quantile of a distribution. Replacing the atom $\delta_{S_{n+1}}$ by $\delta_{+\infty}$ can only raise the quantile, and $S_{n+1}\le \mathrm{Quant}_{1-\alpha}(\sum_{j\le n+1}\tilde\pi_j\delta_{S_j})$ if and only if $S_{n+1}\le \mathrm{Quant}_{1-\alpha}(\sum_{i\le n}\tilde\pi_i\delta_{S_i}+\tilde\pi_{n+1}\delta_{+\infty})$ [@tibshirani2019conformal, Lemma 1]. The right-hand quantile equals $q(Y_{n+1})$ in (10), because the normalisation $\sum_l\phi(y_l)=W+\phi(Y_{n+1})$ is the same. Taking expectations over $\mathcal{E}$ gives $Q\{Y\in\hat C_\phi(X)\}\ge1-\alpha$. Finally, for a fixed set $C$, $Q\{Y\in C(X)\}=\mathbb{E}_P[w(Z)\mathbf{1}\{Y\in C(X)\}]=\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(C)$, so averaging over $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}$ yields $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(\hat C_\phi)]\ge1-\alpha$. $\square$

**Proof of Proposition 4.** Let $C$ satisfy $\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(C)\ge1-\alpha=\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(C^\star_\phi)$ and write $h(x,y)=\phi(y)f(y\,|\, x)$. Pointwise, $(\mathbf{1}\{y\in C^\star_\phi(x)\}-\mathbf{1}\{y\in C(x)\})(h(x,y)-t)\ge0$, because the first factor is positive only where $h\ge t$ and negative only where $h<t$. Integrating over $dy\,dP_X(x)$ gives $\mathbb{E}[\phi(Y)](\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(C^\star_\phi)-\operatorname{Cov}_\phi(C))\ge t\,(\mathbb{E}|C^\star_\phi(X)|-\mathbb{E}|C(X)|)$. The left-hand side is at most zero and $t>0$, so $\mathbb{E}|C^\star_\phi(X)|\le\mathbb{E}|C(X)|$. $\square$
